Check yourself first

Three questions the study page prepared you for. Answer out loud, then open.

1An 8B-parameter model in 16-bit weights: roughly how much GPU memory do the weights take, and what else has to fit?

Show the answer

About 16 GB (this unit's log: 15.27 GiB). Also the KV cache and the working memory the engine measures at its profiling peak.

If you missed it, review GPU memory (VRAM).

2A bootstrap interval on a median: what is resampled, and can the interval reach a value no run took?

Show the answer

The runs, with replacement; each resample gives a median. Yes: a median can fall between two runs, so the interval can span a gap in the data.

If you missed it, review Spread versus interval.

3Why interleave arms in shuffled triples instead of running them in blocks?

Show the answer

Slow drift, such as a busy hour on the rented GPUs, then lands on all three arms instead of posing as an arm effect.

Estimation drills

Do the arithmetic on paper first, then open the worked steps.

KV cache capacity on the 24 GB card

From the log: the card reports 23.52 GiB, vLLM plans with 0.92 of it, weights plus non-torch memory use 15.54 GiB, and the profiling peak is 1.18 GiB (cold) or 0.19 GiB (warm). Assume Qwen3-8B keeps 2-byte K and V for 36 layers, 8 KV heads, head dim 128. How many tokens fit, and how many 8,192-token requests?

Worked answer
  1. Budget: 0.92 × 23.52 = 21.64 GiB.
  2. KV memory: 21.64 − 15.54 − 1.18 = 4.92 GiB cold; 21.64 − 15.54 − 0.19 = 5.91 GiB warm.
  3. Bytes per token: 2 (K and V) × 36 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 147,456 B = 144 KiB (the shape is assumed).
  4. Tokens: 4.92 GiB / 144 KiB ≈ 35,826 against the log's 35,792, which is 2,237 blocks of 16 (block size assumed); 5.91 GiB gives ≈ 43,031 against 43,040. Rounding in the logged GiB explains the gap.
  5. Gain: 0.99 GiB / 144 KiB ≈ 7,209 tokens against the observed 7,248. One GiB of profiling peak costs about 7,282 tokens.
  6. Requests: 35,792 / 8,192 = 4.37 → 4 and 43,040 / 8,192 = 5.25 → 5; at 2,048 tokens, 17 against 21.

Answer. Close to the logged 35,792 and 43,040 tokens: 20.3% more warm, 4 against 5 whole 8,192-token requests, 17 against 21 at 2,048.

Does the weight cache pay for itself?

Use the post's illustrative prices (assumptions, not measured): $1.00 per GPU-hour, $3.50 a month for the volume, 24 scale-ups a day. The weight cache saves 15.60 s per scale-up. At how many scale-ups a day does it break even?

Worked answer
  1. Saving per scale-up: 15.60 s × $1.00 / 3,600 s ≈ $0.00433.
  2. Volume per day: $3.50 / 30.42 days ≈ $0.115.
  3. Break-even: 0.115 / 0.00433 ≈ 26.6 scale-ups a day.
  4. At 24 a day the saving is 24 × 0.00433 ≈ $0.104 a day against $0.115: a small loss, a marginal call.
  5. The compile cache stores for free (26.13 s saved per scale-up, break-even 0.16 a day), but each version change pays a cold compile again.

Answer. About 26.6 scale-ups a day, so 24 a day does not quite pay. Every dollar figure rescales with your own prices.

Deep-dive drill

Ordered easy to hard. Answer out loud, open the model answer, then the red-flag answer: the tempting reply that sounds right and is not.

Drill 1 of 11

Why three arms, and not two or four?

Model answer

A to B changes only the weight cache and B to C only the compile cache, so each saving gets its own one-step comparison. Two arms would test only weights; that was the fallback had this vLLM not compiled at startup, and it does. The fourth cell (weights cold, compile warm) has no operational meaning. A baked-image arm only moves the bytes into the image pull, inside the unsplit platform residual.

Red-flag answer

"More arms is always better", or proposing the baked image without saying its bytes move into a time the study cannot observe.

Drill 2 of 11

What is in platform time, and what is not?

Model answer

Platform time is total cold start minus the worker clock's time: container and handler start, teardown and up to one 5 s poll of lag. It is the one allowed cross-clock subtraction, a residual rather than a measured phase. RunPod's own queue delay was about 0.13 s (median), so it is not queueing.

Red-flag answer

"Platform time is the queue." Or treating it as a measured phase.

Drill 3 of 11

H1 says weight handling is over half of worker-clock time. Does weight loading dominate a cold start?

Model answer

H1 holds by its pre-registered definition: weight handling is a median 0.58 of worker-clock time in arm A, above half in all 100 runs. But that phase runs from launching vllm serve to the "Model loading took" line, so it is mostly server start-up (28.19 s median) beside vLLM's own load (17.40 s of 79.09 s, about 22%). "Weights dominate" claims more than the data shows.

Red-flag answer

"Yes, weights dominate cold start", read straight off the 0.58.

Drill 4 of 11

What is clock A's resolution, and what does it do to your intervals?

Model answer

The laptop learns a job has finished only at a status poll, every 5 s. Clock A's spans repeat on a grid of about 5.175 s (289 of the 299 repeat-host runs lie within 0.25 s of it), so totals move in steps: arm A has runs at 80.92 to 81.16 s and at 85.72 to 85.89 s, none between. That is part of why the interval on the difference is wide. On the worker clock the same difference is −12.16 s [−15.54, −10.74], an exploratory check, not the pre-registered result.

Red-flag answer

"The cloud is noisy." Or swapping in the narrower worker-clock interval after seeing it.

Drill 5 of 11

Why is the compile cache's interval, 25.97 to 31.01 s around 26.07 s, lopsided?

Model answer

B's runs ranked 41 to 56 lie at 65.35 to 65.80 s and rank 57 is 70.29 s, while C's median is steady near 39.4 s. A resample that draws few of B's low runs, about one in ten, lifts B's median into the 70 s cluster: 70.4 − 39.4 = 31.0, the interval's top. The low runs are packed, so the bottom sits only 0.10 s under the estimate. The interval shows where the median can jump, given the gaps in the data.

Red-flag answer

"The bootstrap is broken", or reaching for a t-test because the interval is asymmetric.

Drill 6 of 11

Why does a warm compile cache leave more KV cache? Show the arithmetic, and say what is measured.

Model answer

vLLM plans with 0.92 of the card: 0.92 × 23.52 = 21.64 GiB. The KV cache gets what is left after weights and non-torch memory (15.54 GiB) and the profiling pass's peak: 21.64 − 15.54 − 1.18 = 4.92 GiB after a cold compile, 21.64 − 15.54 − 0.19 = 5.91 GiB after a warm one, 35,792 against 43,040 tokens. It is sized once, so it is fixed for the replica's life. The log shows the drop in peak, not its cause; H5 registered the compiler's memory still being resident during profiling.

Red-flag answer

"The compiler leaks memory", or "it frees up after compile, so it does not matter", or stating the cause as measured.

Drill 7 of 11

Is "one more concurrent request" the right way to state the KV gain?

Model answer

Not as the headline. 43,040 against 35,792 tokens is 20.3% more (7,248 tokens). Whole requests held depend on length: at 8,192 tokens, 4.37 → 4 against 5.25 → 5; at 2,048 tokens, 17.48 → 17 against 21.02 → 21. Report tokens, and give concurrency only for a stated length.

Red-flag answer

"25% more users" (5 against 4), or quoting concurrency without the request length.

Drill 8 of 11

Arm C reaches "model loaded" 8.85 s sooner than B, with only the compile cache changed. What do you check?

Model answer

vLLM's own load time is the same (4.57 vs 4.54 s). The engine log's timestamps put the gap before the engine core starts: the API server's step from the arguments line to architecture resolution takes about 10 s in B and 1 s in C. Arm C warms the whole vLLM cache directory, not only compiled code; a cached model-inspection result is the working guess, not confirmed. A next step would be to warm only part of the directory and watch that gap.

Red-flag answer

"Noise", or "it is the compile", or "unknown" with no next step.

Drill 9 of 11

The volume load took 4.57 s for about 16 GB. Is that the volume's throughput?

Model answer

It would be about 3.6 GB/s, high for network storage. All 300 runs landed on one host, and the first arm B run after a 19.8 h gap spent 17.45 s in vLLM's load, so a warm host cache is one possible reading, unverified. Treat 4.57 s as a warm-host figure until it is measured on fresh hosts.

Red-flag answer

Quoting 4.57 s as the volume's throughput for any host.

Drill 10 of 11

Which cache do you build into a platform, and how do you justify it?

Model answer

Warm the compile cache in the deploy pipeline. It costs nothing to store, but a change of engine version, model, hardware or flags invalidates it, so otherwise the first scale-up after every upgrade pays the cold compile again (18.5 s here). The weight cache is a volume billed continuously: at the post's illustrative prices it breaks even at 26.6 scale-ups a day. Say which numbers are assumptions.

Red-flag answer

"Cache everything on a shared volume" with no invalidation story, or quoting the break-even as a measured result.

Drill 11 of 11

What does the study not tell you about a hundred replicas scaling at once?

Model answer

Nothing: every run was one replica alone, so contention on the volume, registry pulls and many hosts starting together were not tested. The one first-touch run, an image pull on a host that had never seen it, took 2266.6 s, far above every cache effect (15 to 26 s). If scale-up can reach new hosts, image distribution may dominate; that is one observation, not a headline.

Red-flag answer

Extrapolating this study's seconds to fleet behaviour.

Design scenario

You run a serverless inference platform. Customers deploy 7B to 14B open-weight models on 24 GB GPUs. Product wants the first token of a scale-from-zero replica within 30 s at p95, and engine upgrades ship every two weeks. Design the cold-start path, and say what you would measure.

Outline of a strong answer
  1. Decompose before optimising: a worker clock around the engine with log-line timestamps, and a platform clock finer than a 5 s poll. Budget platform, start-up, weight load, compile, profiling, capture, ready and first token.
  2. Compile and engine caches as build artifacts: in CI, start the engine once per engine version, model revision, GPU model and flag set, and publish the cache directory versioned. Check the key at start and alert on a miss. Here a warm directory was worth about 26 s a start and 20.3% more KV cache.
  3. Weights: a volume or node-local copy beat the hub here (vLLM's load 17.40 s against 4.57 s), but 4.57 s may be a warm-host figure. Measure first reads on fresh hosts before promising it, and weigh the volume's fixed cost against scale-up frequency.
  4. Image distribution: the one first-touch run took 2266.6 s, far above every cache effect. Pre-pull on node join and keep the image warm; this study has one observation, not a rate.
  5. Capture and readiness: graph capture is a flat 4 s here, and skipping it (--enforce-eager) saves that per start but costs on every token after, so it suits only short-lived replicas. Gate traffic on /health, and check per engine version that warm-up finishes before it answers (true for 0.27.1 here).
  6. Capacity: report KV tokens per replica as an indicator. A cold compile cost 20.3% of it for the replica's life here.
  7. Measure: p50 and p95 time to first token from zero, split by host warmth; cache-key miss rate after each upgrade; KV tokens per replica; and contention when many replicas start together, which this study did not test.
  8. Trade-offs: the cache matrix grows with GPU models, versions and flags, so build only what the fleet runs. A warm pool costs idle GPU-hours; compare it with the break-even arithmetic.
Pitfalls to check your answer against
  • Quoting this study's seconds for other GPUs, models, engine versions or providers: the seconds do not transfer, the mechanisms do.
  • Promising the weight-cache speed-up from warm-host numbers: all 300 runs used one host.
  • A compile cache with no key check, so a routine upgrade turns it cold in silence.
  • Reporting a p99 or a mean from a hundred starts per set-up.
  • Treating a first-touch image pull as a normal start, or ignoring it.

Lab

No cost

Goal. See the poll grid, the log localisation, the memory line and the exact re-derivation on your own laptop.

An optional paid extension (warming only part of the vLLM cache directory on one rented GPU) would test the cause of the head start; it is not part of this lab and is untested.

Setup

  • A checkout of the repo with the committed data/campaign.jsonl. Use .venv/bin/python and never uv run.
  • No GPU, no network and no paid call.

Steps

  1. Run .venv/bin/python scripts/analyse.py --store data/campaign.jsonl > /tmp/a1_analysis.json, then cmp /tmp/a1_analysis.json data/analysis.json. Expect no output: the file reproduces byte for byte.
  2. Run .venv/bin/python scripts/weights_phase_check.py --store data/campaign.jsonl. Expect start-up before load of 28.19, 25.28 and 16.67 s beside vLLM-reported loads of 17.40, 4.57 and 4.54 s.
  3. Take each run's clock A span (t_total_job from coldstart.analysis.metrics.derive) and fold it modulo trial periods around 5 s. The period with the tightest bunching is about 5.175 s.
  4. Plot an ECDF of t_total per arm over the repeat-host runs (drop the one first-touch run). Expect stairs about 5 s apart.
  5. Call harness.stats.bootstrap_contrast_difference(a, b, c, seed=3) on t_process, then on t_total. Expect about −12.16 [−15.54, −10.74] and −10.39 [−20.30, −5.54].
  6. For each run's engine log lines, take the timestamps of the non-default args line and the Resolved architecture line, and print the median gap per arm. Expect 10, 10 and 1 s.
  7. Read the Desired GPU memory utilization and Actual usage log lines, and recompute KV memory and tokens with the calculator on this page.

Record

  • Byte identity of the analysis file
  • Fitted poll period, and how many of the 299 spans lie within 0.25 s of it (expect 289)
  • Interval widths on the worker clock and the laptop clock
  • Median engine-log gaps per arm
  • KV memory and tokens against the logged 4.92 and 5.91 GiB, 35,792 and 43,040 tokens
Expected result and what it shows

Expected. Everything reproduces from the committed store: identical analysis file, the three start-up figures, a poll grid near 5.175 s, a worker-clock interval about a third as wide as the laptop-clock one, a 10 s, 10 s and 1 s architecture gap, and KV numbers within rounding of the log.

Shows. The clock's resolution sets interval width, the engine log holds more than the summary, and the memory arithmetic closes within rounding.
