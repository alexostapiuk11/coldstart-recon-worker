Estimation drills

Do the arithmetic on paper first, then open the worked steps.

What does a policy cost before you run it?

A 400 s window starts with one replica already running. The controller evaluates every 5 s, acts at most once per 30 s cooldown, adds or removes one replica, and caps at 12; a replica is billed from launch. Traffic starts at t = 0, so the first evaluation that sees work is t = 5 s. Find the cost of (a) utilisation, (b) in flight with up 16 on arm C, (c) queue depth, up 4 and down 1, on arm C.

Worked answer
  1. Utilisation reads 1.0 whenever a replica is busy, so it launches at 5, 35, ..., 305 s: 11 launches, then the cap of 12.
  2. A launch at t is billed 400 − t seconds: 395, 365, ..., 95. Their sum is 11 × 395 − 30 × (0 + 1 + ... + 10) = 4,345 − 1,650 = 2,695.
  3. Add the first replica's 400: 3,095. The cold start never enters, so every utilisation policy costs 3,095.
  4. In flight, up 16: the signal reads 128 per serving replica at 5 s and 35 s, so it launches at 5, 35, ..., 155 s (6 launches), then stops. Billed 395 + 365 + 335 + 305 + 275 + 245 = 1,920, plus 400 is 2,320, the sweep's median for up 16 with down 1, 2 or 4.
  5. Queue depth, up 4 and down 1: replica 2 runs 5 to 95 s (90), replica 3 runs 35 to 65 s (30), replica 4 runs 125 to 215 s (90), replica 5 runs 155 to 185 s (30): 240, plus 400 is 640.

Answer. (a) 3,095, (b) 2,320, (c) 640 replica-seconds. A threshold controller's cost is how many cooldown periods its signal stays past a threshold, which is set by what the signal can see, not by the exact threshold.

What step p99 does a 1.2× overload produce, whatever the signal?

One replica completes 211.2 req/s and arrivals jump to 253.4 req/s, a ratio ρ of 1.2. The first extra replica serves at D = 5 s plus the cold start. Treat the queue as a fluid FIFO queue with a service time of about 0.6 s. Estimate the worst wait for arm C (39.4 s cold start) and arm A (81.1 s), and say what removes it.

Worked answer
  1. A request arriving at t < D finds (λ − μ)t ahead of it, served at μ, so it waits (ρ − 1)t, unless the second replica arrives first.
  2. The worst request is the one whose wait ends exactly at D: (ρ − 1)t = D − t, so t = D/ρ. Its wait is D − D/ρ = D(1 − 1/ρ). After D two replicas drain at 2μ, so later arrivals wait less.
  3. Add the service time: W_max = D(1 − 1/ρ) + 0.6.
  4. Arm C: D = 44.4 s. 44.4 / 6 = 7.4, plus 0.6 is 8.0 s. The simulated medians for in flight and utilisation are 7.20 to 7.73 s: the formula runs 3% to 11% high, since a p99 sits below the maximum.
  5. Arm A: D = 86.1 s. 86.1 / 6 = 14.3, plus 0.6 is 14.9 s, against 14.44 to 15.52 s simulated for all three signals.
  6. Queue depth's second episode on arm C lasts 69 s (95 s to about 164 s): 69 / 6 + 0.6 = 12.1 s, against 11.15 to 12.03 s simulated.
  7. To remove it you need N × μ ≥ λ before the spike: 2 replicas here. No signal can buy that after the spike starts.

Answer. About 8.0 s on arm C and 14.9 s on arm A, whatever the signal: W_max ≈ D(1 − 1/ρ) + 0.6. A shorter cold start nearly halves it; only warm headroom, N × μ ≥ λ, removes it.

Deep-dive drill

Ordered easy to hard. Answer out loud, open the model answer, then the red-flag answer: the tempting reply that sounds right and is not.

Drill 1 of 12

At the 0.70× baseline of 147.8 req/s, about how many requests are in flight on one replica, and how do you know without running anything?

Model answer

About 66. Little's Law: in flight = rate × latency, roughly 147.8 req/s × 0.44 s. The measured curve completes only 139.5 req/s at 64 in flight, so the baseline sits just above 64. A 2 to 16 in-flight grid is therefore crossed by 45.5 req/s (about a fifth of capacity) and fires at baseline.

Red-flag answer

"About 16, the top of the grid", or a guess from GPU memory.

Drill 2 of 12

Why does nvidia-smi utilisation read 1.0 with a single request in flight?

Model answer

As NVIDIA defines the counter (check the docs for your driver), it is the share of the sample period in which any kernel was running, not how much of the GPU the kernels use. One request's decode steps keep a kernel running almost continuously (my explanation, not separately measured), so it reads 1.0 from 3.5 req/s, 1.7% of 204.4. Whether a graded counter such as SM activity helps here was not measured.

Red-flag answer

"One request really uses the whole GPU", or "it is a sampling bug".

Drill 3 of 12

What does queue depth read at 90% load on this engine, and what does that do to a scale-down threshold of 0 or 1?

Model answer

About zero in the fluid picture: requests wait only once all 128 slots are running, so a queue appears past 204.4 req/s (bursty arrivals queue a little earlier). A down threshold of 0 to 1 therefore fires whenever capacity is merely enough, and removes the replica that made it enough. That is the simulated thrash in the walkthrough.

Red-flag answer

"Queue depth is proportional to load."

Drill 4 of 12

Why can a per-serving-replica signal not fall while a new replica is starting, and why does that cost money?

Model answer

The signal divides by serving replicas. A starting replica is billed from launch but not counted, so per-replica load stays high and the controller adds one more per cooldown until enough have arrived to pull the signal under its threshold. That is why utilisation, always 1.0, climbs to the 12-replica cap.

Red-flag answer

"Starting replicas add capacity at once, so it settles quickly."

Drill 5 of 12

All 19 queue-depth policies cost 640 replica-seconds on arm C. Why?

Model answer

The queue reads 0 or hundreds (196 to 2,546 in the replayed run, otherwise 0), so nearly every up threshold from 1 to 16 and down threshold from 0 to 1 makes the same moves: UP at 5 and 35 s, DOWN at 65 and 95, UP at 125 and 155, DOWN at 185 and 215. 410 of 570 replayed runs make exactly those eight; the rest add a launch. Cost is launch-time arithmetic: 400 + 240 = 640.

Red-flag answer

"The thresholds were well chosen", or "the simulator is broken".

Drill 6 of 12

Estimate in-flight's and utilisation's step p99 on arm C without the simulator.

Model answer

The first extra replica serves at D = 5 s + 39.4 s = 44.4 s. At 1.2× overload the worst wait is about D(1 − 1/1.2) + 0.6 = 8.0 s; the simulated medians are 7.20 to 7.73 s, since a p99 sits below the maximum. Arm A: D = 86.1 s gives 14.9 s against 14.44 to 15.52 s. The dead time sets it, not the signal.

Red-flag answer

"The p99 comes from the service curve, about 0.6 s."

Drill 7 of 12

On the step, the simulated gap grew from arm A to arm C, against H3. Explain it without saying faster cold starts make the signal matter more.

Model answer

On arm C the first new replica serves at about 44 s and the backlog clears, so queue depth scales down twice with the spike still on and pays a second cold start: a 69 s single-replica overload, p99 about 12 s. On arm A the replayed run has two or more replicas serving by its first scale-down, and all three signals sit between 14.44 and 15.52 s (gap 0.082 s). A rerun with scale-down off came close to the others (exploratory, not committed).

Red-flag answer

"A faster cold start makes the signal matter more", stated as a finding.

Drill 8 of 12

Would Kubernetes' HPA have produced this thrash?

Model answer

I did not test it. A proportional autoscaler computes a desired count from the signal and typically has separate scale-up and scale-down timing, with a long scale-down window by default (check your version). This simulator removes one replica per action under one shared 30 s cooldown, a modelling choice, so its frontiers describe this controller, not any real autoscaler.

Red-flag answer

"HPA has a cooldown too, so it is the same."

Drill 9 of 12

Why does the gate judge the arrivals the engine received rather than the send schedule, and what did that cost?

Model answer

RunPod's load balancer held requests and released them in bursts, so judged by send time its stalls would count as simulator misses. The cost: the prediction can no longer be written before the run, and the bursts, up to 512 requests at the engine with at most 128 running, become model input in a regime the curve never measured.

Red-flag answer

"To make the model pass."

Drill 10 of 12

Attempt one missed by 14% in the median but by up to 89% in some bins. Reconcile the two.

Model answer

Near saturation, waiting time depends on arrival rate minus capacity, a small difference of two large numbers. The replay peaked at 0.98× of measured throughput and the load balancer's bursts pushed the engine past its 128 cap, so a modest speed difference can turn a backlog the simulator predicts into none. Consistent with the data, not isolated: the quiet bins missed by 14%, which host speed explains only in part.

Red-flag answer

"The −89% bins are outliers", or "host speed explains everything".

Drill 11 of 12

Is the miss rule fair to a perfect model? Quantify it.

Model answer

Per bin, three unbiased repeats land on one side with probability 2 × (1/2)³ = 1/4, the same as the prediction falling outside the three runs' min–max. The pass rule needs 10 judged bins and at most half missing. For independent bins a perfect model fails about 2% at 10 bins and under 0.1% at 30; a model outside the spread in 3 bins of 4 fails 92% and 99.7%. Correlated bins and host effects raise the false fail to about 2.5% and near 5% (the pre-registration's simulation).

Red-flag answer

"A good model should hit every bin."

Drill 12 of 12

What would you measure to answer the original question for real?

Model answer

A closed-loop run on real replicas with a real autoscaler replaying recorded spikes; the load balancer's scaler value set to the engine's cap and its routing measured; host speed recorded per replica; validation in the overload regime the sweep uses; an asymmetric scale-down; and p99 with replica-seconds over many spikes. The simulator then drops out of the claim.

Red-flag answer

"Run the simulator with more repetitions."

Design scenario

You run Qwen3-8B on vLLM on Kubernetes, on RTX 4090-class nodes. A new pod takes 40 to 80 s to start serving. Traffic is diurnal with unannounced bursts of up to 30% that last a few minutes. The SLO is p99 under 3 s at minimum cost. Design the autoscaling: what you scale on, how you set it, how you know it works.

Outline of a strong answer
  1. Measure one replica first: a concurrency sweep for latency, throughput and the memory ceiling, on your own request shapes. The toy shape here (13-token prompts, 16 output tokens) gives 204.4 req/s per replica; yours will differ a lot.
  2. Size headroom from the dead time, not from the signal. A burst of ρ times a fleet's capacity builds a backlog for about the cold start D, with worst wait about D(1 − 1/ρ), if one new replica restores capacity (else add a cooldown per extra launch).
  3. For ρ = 1.3 that is 0.23 D, about 15 s at D = 65 s, far over a 3 s SLO. So keep warm capacity of at least 1.3× typical load, or cut D (weights on fast storage, compile caches).
  4. Scale up on requests in flight per replica, with a target taken from your own curve at the concurrency whose latency still meets the SLO with room for a burst. Never on nvidia-smi utilisation: it read 1.0 from one request.
  5. Use waiting requests only as a fast overload trigger. It reads 0 until a replica is full, so it cannot ask for capacity early, and it must not drive scale-down.
  6. Scale down slowly and asymmetrically: a window longer than a burst plus a cold start, never on an empty queue alone. This simulator removed the newest replica first, even one still starting, and paid a second cold start.
  7. Mechanics: your platform's autoscaler fed by the engine's running and waiting request counts (check metric names and defaults for your versions); minimum replicas from the headroom rule; check the load balancer's per-worker cap and routing (RunPod's default capped two workers at 17.1 req/s).
  8. Validate closed loop: replay recorded bursts against a staging fleet with the real autoscaler and record p99, replica-seconds, scale events and the host of each pod. Trust an offline simulator only after it passes a pre-registered gate, in the overload regime too.
Pitfalls to check your answer against
  • Quoting a simulated gap or signal ranking as a finding: the simulator failed its gate twice.
  • Scaling on nvidia-smi utilisation, which read 1.0 from one request.
  • Symmetric scale-down on an empty queue: the simulated queue-depth policy gave capacity back mid-spike and paid a second cold start.
  • Sizing from one host's curve: the other hosts measured ran 7 to 18% faster than the curve's host under load.
  • Copying the toy numbers (204.4 req/s, 13-token prompts) into production sizing.

Runs on your machine

Goal. See the control loop act, derive its costs from launch times, and test whether queue depth's simulated disadvantage on arm C comes from its scale-down.
