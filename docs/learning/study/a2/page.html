Study page · about 45 min

An autoscaling simulator that failed its own test

Read first: LLM serving on a GPU · How these experiments earn trust

Which autoscaling signal, queue depth (requests waiting per replica), requests in flight per replica or GPU utilisation, gives the best cost and p99 trade-off in a traffic spike? Does a faster cold start change the answer? The simulator built to answer it failed its own validation gate twice.

Answer first

I tried to find which autoscaling signal, queue depth, requests in flight or GPU utilisation, gives the best cost and tail-latency trade-off for an LLM server whose new replicas take tens of seconds to over a minute to start. Live tests were too expensive, so I built a simulator from measured inputs with a pre-registered test it had to pass. It failed twice: it first predicted latency too high, which I traced partly to host speed, then, calibrated to the host, too low, for a reason I could not settle. So I published the failure, and two measured findings: GPU utilisation reads 100% from one request, and RunPod's load balancer caps each worker at 4 requests in flight by default.

Need to know first

C1The service curve

One replica's latency, throughput and GPU utilisation at fixed numbers of requests in flight, 1 to 128, three runs each; 256 ran out of GPU memory. The simulator reads service times from it instead of modelling continuous batching.
Used by: D1 Answer in a simulator, held to a gate · D5 Validate the engine, not the platform

C2Little's Law

In flight = arrival rate × time each request spends. At 128 in flight and 0.606 s each, one replica completes 128 / 0.606 = 211.2 req/s: the "saturation" every traffic rate here is a fraction of.
Used by: D4 Choose the traffic by a ranking-blind rule

C3Why GPU utilisation is blind here

nvidia-smi reads 1.0 from one request upward and 0 only when idle. A utilisation autoscaler sees "fully busy" whenever a replica serves anything, so it adds replicas at every chance up to its cap: busy and drowning look the same.
Used by: D2 One controller, a grid per signal, frontiers

C4Frontier, dominance and the iso-cost slice

A policy is a signal plus its up and down thresholds; over 30 simulated spikes it gives a median cost (replica-seconds, billed from each launch) and a median per-run p99. It is dominated if another costs no more, has no worse p99 and is better on one; the rest are the frontier. The iso-cost budget is the cheapest spend at which every signal has a policy; the gap is the worst signal's p99 there minus the best's. H3: the gap at least halves from arm A to arm C, on step and ramp.
Used by: D2 One controller, a grid per signal, frontiers · D4 Choose the traffic by a ranking-blind rule

C5The miss rule

Per 10 s bin, each real repeat's residual is its p50 minus its own prediction. A bin misses when all three residuals sit on one side by more than 1 ms. With no bias each repeat is a coin flip, so a perfect model misses at most 2 × (1/2)³ = 1/4 of bins. Pass: at least 10 judged bins, at most half missing.
Used by: D3 Tolerance from reality's own repeatability · D5 Validate the engine, not the platform · D6 One disclosed fix, then publish the failure

C6Host speed

Rented GPUs of one model are not equally fast, and RunPod picks the host. Every other host measured had 4 to 18% lower latency than the curve's, more at high load: about the size of the gate's misses (14% and 13%). A measured cause, not shown to be the whole one.
Used by: D6 One disclosed fix, then publish the failure

Requirements that drove everything

R1Measure inputs; model only control

Cold-start lag, latency under load and GPU utilisation come from hardware; only the autoscaler's control loop is simulated.
The gate then tests exactly the modelled part. Assuming how utilisation maps to load would invent the effect under test.
Answered by: D1 Answer in a simulator, held to a gate · D5 Validate the engine, not the platform

R2Compare frontiers, not tuned points

Judge each signal by every cost/p99 trade-off its threshold grid reaches, not by one tuning.
Every policy has thresholds, and whichever set you publish decides the winner.
Answered by: D2 One controller, a grid per signal, frontiers

R3Vary only the cold start: A vs C

Arm A (nothing cached, median 81.07 s) and arm C (fully cached, 39.37 s) differ only in cold-start lag; spike and traffic stay fixed.
H3 asks whether a faster cold start shrinks the gap between signals; anything else moving with the arm would confound it. (The A1 unit's 81.12 s also counts arm A's one image-pull run.)
Answered by: D4 Choose the traffic by a ranking-blind rule

R4No answer before the model passes

The simulator must reproduce a real replica's latency under a test fixed in advance; if it fails, publish the failure, not the answer.
Tuning a simulator until it agrees is "fabrication with extra steps" (the design spec); a failed gate is a result.
Answered by: D1 Answer in a simulator, held to a gate · D3 Tolerance from reality's own repeatability · D5 Validate the engine, not the platform · D6 One disclosed fix, then publish the failure

R5Rules before data; dated amendments

Hypotheses, grids, control parameters, the traffic rule and the pass rule are committed before the sweep or validation they govern, except H1, H2 and H4, which were defined after the frontiers were seen; later changes are dated amendments saying what had been seen.
Otherwise the traffic could be tuned until a signal wins, or the tolerance until the model passes.
Answered by: D3 Tolerance from reality's own repeatability · D4 Choose the traffic by a ranking-blind rule · D6 One disclosed fix, then publish the failure

R6Built offline, small paid runs

Build and test the simulator, sweep and gate arithmetic with no GPU; keep paid runs small and stoppable; re-derive every number on a laptop.
Most of it was buildable offline. Estimate $35–60; billed $6.07, partly because the closed-loop run never happened.
Answered by: no decision. Met by process: "Build the simulator with no GPU" and "Commit the pass rule" come before any paid run, and one script re-derives every published number from committed files.

Core decisions

D1Answer in a simulator, held to a gate

Serves: R1 Measure inputs; model only control · R4 No answer before the model passes
Seen in: F1 The service curve: latency as load rises
Problem
The question needs dozens of policies run thirty times each through real spikes: out of budget on rented GPUs.
Options
Run the policies live on real replicasA model of vLLM's scheduler in place of the measured curveA simulator trusted without a testA simulator fed only measured inputs, gated first
Because
Live tests cost too much and modelling the scheduler would assume what the curve measures, so measured inputs feed a gated simulator.
Cost
No answer if the gate fails; it failed twice.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
Modelling the scheduler would replace a measurement with an assumption about what continuous batching does to latency; the curve measures it, and only the control loop is code. Discrete events need no tick size, and a seed makes runs reproducible. A new replica is absent until a lag resampled from artifact 1's runs, then fully serving.
Cost
If the gate fails there is no answer, and it failed twice. The design's second gate, one real closed-loop autoscaling run, was never built.

D2One controller, a grid per signal, frontiers

Serves: R2 Compare frontiers, not tuned points
Seen in: F6 UNVALIDATED: what each policy spent and reached (arm C, step)
Problem
Any published tuning decides the winner, and the signals do not share units: utilisation is a fraction, the others are counts.
Options
Hand-pick each signal's best thresholdsOne shared grid, 2 to 16, for all threeOne controller, a grid per signal in its own units; compare frontiers
Because
Published thresholds decide the winner, so each signal is swept over a grid in its own units.
Cost
Grids fixed on a placeholder curve; on the measured one each frontier is a point and the budget never binds.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
A shared 2 to 16 grid puts every threshold above utilisation's maximum of 1, so it never scales and H2 "wins" by a units mismatch (checked: 0 scale-ups at 2.0). Fixed for every policy: a 30 s cooldown (one pause after any action, up or down), a 5 s evaluation, a 12-replica cap, 30 repetitions.
Cost
Grids were fixed on 2026-09-05 against a placeholder curve that stopped at 64 in flight, and not revisited. On the measured curve each signal's step frontier is one point, and the iso-cost budget, 3,095 replica-seconds, is utilisation's floor: it never constrains the other two.

D3Tolerance from reality's own repeatability

Serves: R4 No answer before the model passes · R5 Rules before data; dated amendments
Seen in: F3 The test, twice: which side the engine landed on
Problem
How close must the simulator be? The bar has to be fixed before any real run.
Options
A chosen tolerance, say 5%Compare against a fresh random draw of the trafficRequire every bin inside the real runs' bandLeave the number of repeats openThree repeats of the exact trace; at most half the 10 s bins may miss
Because
A model cannot be more reproducible than the system, so three real repeats set the tolerance.
Cost
Correlated bins make a perfect model fail more often than stated; two attempts at most.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
A model cannot be required to be more reproducible than the system it models, so three real repeats set the tolerance, on the exact trace so traffic randomness cannot pose as model error. A perfect model still misses up to a quarter of bins, so "every bin" would often fail it; a model biased beyond the system's own spread misses most bins (the pre-registration's example, three bins in four, fails 92% of the time at 10 bins). Exactly three repeats: a min–max range only widens with more.
Cost
Correlated bins make a perfect model fail more often than the stated 2% at 10 bins (2.5% simulated, near 5% with host effects). p50 suited bins of a few hundred requests; the replay's held over a thousand. Two attempts at most.

D4Choose the traffic by a ranking-blind rule

Serves: R3 Vary only the cold start: A vs C · R5 Rules before data; dated amendments
Seen in: F2 Throughput, and the traffic set from it
Problem
As registered, the spike peaked at 3.4× one replica's capacity and all 55 policies gave the same p99 to nine decimals. Later, on the measured curve, the replacement starved queue depth.
Options
Keep the registered traffic and report the tieRaise the replica capWiden the threshold grids upwardSearch by a measure that cannot prefer a signal
Because
As registered, all 55 policies gave one p99; counting distinct outcomes cannot prefer a signal.
Cost
Choosing traffic after seeing data is a disclosed hazard; the gate never tested the sweep's overload.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
Past every threshold from the first evaluation, every signal says "scale up" at once, and with an 81 s cold start no replica arrives in time, so only cost differs. A bigger cap never binds; wider grids would redefine each signal's range. A count of distinct outcomes cannot pick a winner, and the first re-choice refuted the hypotheses, which a rigged choice would not.
Cost
Choosing traffic after seeing data is a real hazard: per-policy p99s had been printed before the second search's criteria were written, as the amendment says. The gate stayed at 0.95× capacity while the sweep moved to 1.20×.

D5Validate the engine, not the platform

Serves: R4 No answer before the model passes · R1 Measure inputs; model only control
Seen in: F5 The load balancer's default cap: offered against delivered
Problem
The replica sits behind RunPod's load balancer, which, the probes found, caps, routes, stalls and drops requests in ways the simulator does not model.
Options
RunPod's queue API (/run) for the replayTwo pinned replicas, as first signedJudge client latency, or bin by send timeRetry failures until they succeedOne replica, judged on what its engine received; an unanswered 502 retried once
Because
The load balancer fills one worker first and stalls, so the gate judges what the engine received.
Cost
No prediction before the run; even splitting across replicas never tested.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
The queue API allows 1,000 requests per 10 s, far below the replay's planned peak of about 400 req/s. The load balancer fills one worker before the next, so two replicas would score its routing against the simulator's even split; it holds and releases bursts, so send times would score its stalls as model misses. Retrying until success hides failures.
Cost
The prediction can no longer be written before the run, and the bursts become model input: the engine held more than its 128 running cap, up to 512 counting requests queued inside it. Even splitting across replicas stays untested.

D6One disclosed fix, then publish the failure

Serves: R4 No answer before the model passes · R5 Rules before data; dated amendments
Seen in: F3 The test, twice: which side the engine landed on · F4 Host speed: one GPU model, different latency
Problem
Attempt one failed; an exploratory re-measurement pointed to host speed. What may change without bending the test?
Options
Keep adjusting until it passesFit the curve to the validation runsPublish the frontiers with a caveatOne disclosed fix, declared the last attempt; then publish the failure
Because
Tuning until it agrees is fabrication: one disclosed fix, declared the last, then the failure is the result.
Cost
No measured answer; the over-correction is unexplained; H1, H2, H4 were defined after seeing frontiers.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
Tuning until it agrees is what R4 forbids. The fix was two numbers per repeat, latency ratios at 64 and 128 in flight measured on the same worker before its replay, on traffic the gate never judges. It failed the other way by about as much (the engine 14% faster than predicted, then 13% slower).
Cost
No measured answer. Why the calibration over-corrected is an untested hypothesis (closed-loop calibration, open-loop bursty replay; one host). H1, H2 and H4 were defined only after the frontiers were seen, and are labelled so.

F1The service curve: latency as load rises

Whiskers: lowest and highest of the three runs. At 256 in flight the engine died (out of GPU memory) in 3 of 3 runs, so the curve stops at 128.

Axis
Measured: one replica of vLLM serving Qwen3-8B on an RTX 4090, median end-to-end latency at each fixed number of requests in flight.
Read it as
Latency only doubles from 1 to 128 in flight while throughput grows about 58-fold: what continuous batching buys. The simulator reads every service time from this one host's curve.
How it is computed
Each point is the median of three runs' medians; 0.606 / 0.284 = 2.1.

F2Throughput, and the traffic set from it

GPU utilisation (nvidia-smi) read 1.0 at every level from 1 to 128, and 0 when idle, so it is not drawn: it would be a flat line at the top.

Axis
Measured: requests one replica completes per second at each fixed number in flight. Dashed: traffic levels set from it, as multiples of saturation (211.2 req/s).
Read it as
The validation replay peaked at 0.95× saturation (200.6 req/s), which is 0.98× of the 204.4 measured at 128 in flight; the sweep's spike peaked at 1.20× saturation (253.4 req/s) and stayed there, a regime the gate never tested.
How it is computed
Saturation by Little's Law: 128 / 0.606 s = 211.2 req/s (measured at 128: 204.4). Baseline 0.70 × 211.2 = 147.8; peaks add 0.25 or 0.5 × 211.2: 200.6 and 253.4 req/s. 200.6 / 204.4 = 0.98 of measured throughput.

The budget line constrains no one, so H3's 3.96 s "gap" is queue depth spending 640 against 2,320–3,095. Queue depth is cheap because the simulated controller scales back down once the backlog clears; a read-only rerun with scale-down switched off (exploratory, not committed) brought its p99 close to the others', so the gap mostly reflects the scale-down rule, not the scale-up signal.
How it is computed
Gap = worst reached p99 − best at the budget: 11.15 − 7.20 = 3.95 s on rounded values (post: 3.96, 95% interval 3.52–4.56). Arm A's step: 0.082 s, so H3's halving fails.

Interview card

30 seconds. I tried to find which autoscaling signal, queue depth, requests in flight or GPU utilisation, gives the best cost and tail-latency trade-off for an LLM server whose new replicas take tens of seconds to over a minute to start. Live tests were too expensive, so I built a simulator from measured inputs with a pre-registered test it had to pass. It failed twice: it first predicted latency too high, which I traced partly to host speed, then, calibrated to the host, too low, for a reason I could not settle. So I published the failure, and two measured findings: GPU utilisation reads 100% from one request, and RunPod's load balancer caps each worker at 4 requests in flight by default.
The 2-minute version

A new LLM replica takes a median 39 to 81 seconds to start, so the signal an autoscaler watches has to see trouble early. I compared queue depth, requests in flight and GPU utilisation by their whole cost-versus-p99 frontiers, and asked whether a faster cold start shrinks the difference. Dozens of policies run thirty times each was out of budget on real GPUs, so I built a simulator where every input is measured and only the control loop is code, and committed a pass rule before any real run: replay a real spike three times; a 10-second bin misses if all three runs land on one side of the prediction; more than half missing fails. It failed with the engine 14% faster than predicted. A re-measurement ruled out the engine flag and showed hosts differ by about that much, so host speed is part of the cause. I made one disclosed fix, declared it the last attempt, and it failed 13% the other way. So I published the failure instead of the frontiers. Two findings are measured: nvidia-smi utilisation reads 1.0 from one request, and RunPod's load balancer caps requests per worker at a setting documented only for autoscaling, which made each request 18 times dearer. The paid part cost $6.07.

Hard follow-ups

So which signal should I use?

Not answered by measurement. Measured: nvidia-smi utilisation reads 1.0 from one request up, so all 19 simulated utilisation policies ran to the cap. The unvalidated simulator puts the three within 82 ms on arm A's step, queue depth spending 880 replica-seconds against 2,535–3,095. Practically, on this stack: don't scale on nvidia-smi utilisation; check the load balancer first.

Why trust a simulator at all?

By default I didn't. 55 policies × 30 repetitions × 4 sweeps is 6,600 simulated spikes, unaffordable live, so it had to pass a rule committed on 2026-10-03, before any real run. It failed, and that is what I published.

Wasn't the calibration just moving the goalposts?

One disclosed fix was allowed, and the amendment declared it the last attempt before it ran: two numbers per repeat, from traffic the gate never judges. It failed the other way by about as much, which is the finding: the host moves latency as much as the model's error.

You changed the traffic twice after seeing data. Isn't that p-hacking?

The registered spike gave every policy one p99: no question to answer. Both re-choices used measures that cannot prefer a signal, and the second search's criteria were committed before its code. I concede that per-policy p99s had been seen (the amendment says so), and the gate never tested the overload the sweep ran in.

What would a careful reviewer flag in your own write-up?

The gap's interval is called paired, but the code pairs only queue depth with in-flight at shared thresholds; utilisation runs on other traces. H3's wording says "p99-damage spread", the spec's integral over an SLO, but the analysis uses each run's p99 and no SLO exists. And the post omits the request shape (random 13-token prompts, 16 output tokens) and the absent-or-serving replica model.

What would you do differently?

Measure host speed per run from the start; settle the load balancer before the gate; validate in the sweep's overload; build the closed-loop gate. And revisit the grids: in-flight's tops out at 16, while one replica holds over 64 in flight at baseline alone (139.5 req/s at 64, under the 147.8 baseline). My untested reading: every in-flight threshold is crossed at once, so its grid only sets where scaling stops; up-2 and up-4 hit the cap.

Limits: do not claim more. No measured answer: every frontier, H1–H4 verdict, gap and per-signal dollar figure is from a simulator that failed validation twice, and H1, H2, H4 were defined after the frontiers were seen. The control loop was never compared with a real autoscaler. The gate tested 0.95× saturation (0.98× of measured throughput), the sweep ran at 1.20×. Even load splitting is assumed; RunPod fills one worker first. One provider, GPU, model, engine version (vLLM 0.27.1) and request shape; replicas absent or serving; 6 hosts seen, 3 timed. Load-balancer findings: five unregistered probes on 2026-10-05.

Recall check

Answer out loud first, then open.

Which requirement breaks if the frontiers are published although the gate failed?

R4, no answer before the model passes: every number would be an unvalidated model's, presented as a finding.

Why does a perfect model still miss about one bin in four, and how does the pass rule allow for it?

Unbiased, each repeat lands above or below its prediction like a coin, so all three on one side has probability at most 2 × 1/8 = 1/4, less with the 1 ms margin. The rule passes with at least 10 judged bins and at most half missing.

Attempt one missed with the engine faster, attempt two, calibrated, with it slower. What does the pair let you conclude?

The model's error is about the size of a host's effect (14% against 13%), so a one-host curve cannot predict the hosts RunPod picks. Host speed is a measured cause, not shown to be the whole one, and the pair does not say which way a given host goes.

Why do two failures mean the answer is not in this study, rather than "try a third fix"?

The calibration was signed as the second and last attempt before it ran; a third fix after two failures is the tuning-until-it-agrees R4 forbids.

Why does the gate judge the arrivals the engine received, not the driver's send times?

RunPod's load balancer held requests and released bursts, so the engine never received the schedule. Judged by send time, the platform's stalls would count as simulator misses.

Why is GPU utilisation blind on this engine, and what did it do to its policies?

nvidia-smi reads 1.0 from one request up, above every threshold (highest 0.95), so it scales up at every chance: all 19 policies ran at the 12-replica cap.

As registered, all 55 policies gave the same p99. Why, and why not just raise the replica cap?

At 3.4× capacity every signal is past every threshold from the first evaluation and no replica arrives within the 81 s cold start, so all act alike. The cap never bound: 12, 24 and 64 gave identical results.

On arm C the simulated gap is 3.96 s. Why is that not a clean signal effect?

The budget, utilisation's floor (3,095), never constrains the other two, so the gap compares queue depth at 640 replica-seconds with signals spending 2,320–3,095.

Which requirement does holding the spike fixed across arms protect, and in what unit is the spike set?

R3, vary only the cold start. Its rates are fractions of one replica's Little's-law saturation, 211.2 req/s, and its 190 s length is the same for both arms.
