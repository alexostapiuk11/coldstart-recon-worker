The anatomy of a matching engine's tail

Read first: How these experiments earn trust

Under open-loop load on commodity hardware, where do a deterministic Java matching pipeline's slowest 0.01% of messages spend their time, how much do the hand-off design and the durability policy move that tail, and how much of it is the JVM launch and the host? Three rented bare-metal servers, one JVM per run, no network; nothing comparable to kernel-bypass or FPGA systems.

Answer first

I built a deterministic Java matching engine, ran it open-loop as one in-process pipeline on three rented bare-metal servers, and attributed its slowest messages to causes, with rules fixed before the campaign. The lead finding: which hand-off decision sets the tail depends on load. At low load, spinning versus parking matters more than ring versus queue; at 500,000 messages a second the queue's tail goes to milliseconds while the ring's stays in single microseconds. And once an fdatasync is on the response path, waiting for it is the whole tail.

Need to know first

C1Coordinated omission, open versus closed loop

Closed loop: send the next request when the last response arrives. During a stall it sends one request instead of the many that were due, so the stall is sampled once and the tail under-reported (Gil Tene named this). Open loop: send times are fixed in advance and latency runs from the intended time, so a generator that falls behind counts against the system.
Used by: K2 Open-loop from a fixed schedule, stamped in one clock

C2HdrHistogram and what a p99.99 rests on

p99.99: the latency 99.99% of messages stay at or under. HdrHistogram keeps 3 significant digits over a huge range and merges without loss. A percentile is reported only with at least 100 samples above it: 2,000,000 messages leave 200 above p99.99 but 20 above p99.999, so this study reports nothing past p99.99.
Used by: K2 Open-loop from a fixed schedule, stamped in one clock · K6 The launch is the unit; intervals over rounds

C3The Disruptor ring, the queue, and wait strategies

Ring buffer: a preallocated array with sequence counters; one producer and one consumer allocate nothing; with busy-spin they take no lock (the blocking strategy enters a monitor per publish). ArrayBlockingQueue: one lock for both ends; a thread that must wait (lock held, or an empty queue in the park arm) enqueues a wait node (allocates) and parks. Wait strategy: what an idle consumer does, busy-spin (poll in a loop, burning a core) or park (sleep until woken).
Used by: K3 Split the hand-off into structure and wait strategy

C4Page-cache write, fdatasync, group commit

write lands in the page cache: it survives a killed JVM, not a kernel crash or power loss. fdatasync returns once data is on stable storage; a drive with a volatile write cache must flush first (these report none, VWC 0). D0 no journal; D1 page-cache write, response not gated; D2 group commit: up to 4,096 entries per fdatasync; D3 one fdatasync per order.
Used by: K1 Journal beside the matcher; release gated by sequence number · K4 Sweep durability where per-order sync can keep up

C5JVM safepoints and G1 pauses

Some JVM operations stop every Java thread: GC pauses, some deoptimisation. So a thread that allocates nothing still stops when another thread's allocation triggers a collection; a handshake acts on one thread without stopping the rest. Here: G1 with one GC thread on the housekeeping core. The generator stops too, but latency runs from the intended time, so the stop counts.
Used by: K3 Split the hand-off into structure and wait strategy · K5 Credit causes by coverage of the excess, against a shifted null

C6Deterministic replay and the hash gate

One thread over a totally ordered input, depending on nothing else: replaying the input rebuilds the same state. Breakers: hash-ordered iteration, clock reads, floating-point prices, threads in the core; the engine has none. The matcher's rolling output hash, checkpointed every million sequence numbers, must reappear when a fresh matcher replays the journal.
Used by: K1 Journal beside the matcher; release gated by sequence number

C7Tail attribution against a shifted null

Excess: a tail message's latency minus the fork's median. Busy period: back to when the in-flight count last rose above its median. A cause is credited if it covers at least 50% of the excess: durability wait and matcher path from the message's own stamps; safepoints and sentinel gaps (idle polls over 5 µs apart) by time in the busy period, checked against a null of 200 circular shifts. Credits are non-exclusive; uncredited is residual.
Used by: K5 Credit causes by coverage of the excess, against a shifted null

Requirements that drove everything

R1Attribute the tail, name the rest

Credit each message in a run's slowest 0.01% to measured causes, test inferred causes against a null, and publish what no cause covers as a residual.
A search of public writing found latency trees with no distributions (Coinbase) and engines that exclude journaling (exchange-core), not an attributed p99.99 with its blind part named.
Answered by: K5 Credit causes by coverage of the excess, against a shifted null

R2Open-loop, one clock, no network

Score every message from its intended send time on a schedule fixed in advance, with every stamp from one host's clock and no network.
A generator that waits for responses under-samples stalls (coordinated omission); stamps from different clocks cannot be subtracted.
Answered by: K2 Open-loop from a fixed schedule, stamped in one clock

R3One swapped object per experiment

E1 swaps only the Channel (all three hand-offs at once); E2 swaps only the Durability, its hand-off fixed at ring + busy-spin in advance.
Then any difference comes from that one object; picking E2's hand-off from E1's winner would make the design depend on data.
Answered by: K1 Journal beside the matcher; release gated by sequence number · K3 Split the hand-off into structure and wait strategy · K4 Sweep durability where per-order sync can keep up

R4Rules fixed before the campaign

Commit H1 to H6 with thresholds and decision rules, plus exclusion, stopping and headline rules, before the first campaign fork.
Six of eight verdicts came out fails or mixed; only a timestamped pre-registration shows they were not chosen after the campaign's data.
Answered by: K4 Sweep durability where per-order sync can keep up · K5 Credit causes by coverage of the excess, against a shifted null · K6 The launch is the unit; intervals over rounds

R5Intervals over launches

The JVM launch on one host is the unit: contrasts within rounds, intervals over rounds, per host and pooled.
Messages from one JVM share its JIT decisions, memory layout and host; an interval over them looks tight and means little.
Answered by: K6 The launch is the unit; intervals over rounds

R6Correctness checked, every fork

Every fork: replay reproduces the live output hashes, and at D2 and D3 no response leaves before its order is durable; host settings read back every round.
A fast wrong engine, a leaky gate or a silently reverted setting would void every latency number.
Answered by: K1 Journal beside the matcher; release gated by sequence number

System views (C4)

One fork: five threads, three swapped channels

One JVM per fork, one thread per isolated core. The wire ring is fixed; E1 swaps the three Channels, E2 the Durability. Output releases when matched and, at D2 and D3, once the durable sequence reaches the order: latency is the slower branch, not the sum.

Core decisions

K1Journal beside the matcher; release gated by sequence number

Serves: R3 One swapped object per experiment · R6 Correctness checked, every fork
Problem
The journal must sit somewhere relative to matching, and the queue and the ring must be swappable in the same places.
Options
LMAX multicast: one ring, matcher and journaler consuming the same slotsSerial write-ahead: gateway, journal, matcher, output in a lineSymmetric channels: the gateway fans out to matcher and journaler at every level; output waits for both at D2 and D3
Because
A multicast ring cannot hold a queue arm; serial write-ahead inflates durability's penalty; beside the matcher, latency is the slower branch.
Cost
D0 and D1 can release an order a process crash then loses; power loss was never tested.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
ArrayBlockingQueue cannot multicast, so a multicast ring would give the queue arms another topology, not one swapped object. Serial write-ahead puts durability on the path and inflates its penalty. Beside the matcher, latency is the slower branch, and D0 keeps the fan-out, so D0 against D1 isolates the journal I/O.
Cost
At D0 and D1 a response can leave before its order reaches the page cache, so a process crash can lose an answered order (3 of 10 laptop kills at D1 failed the crash check). Power loss was not tested.

K2Open-loop from a fixed schedule, stamped in one clock

A Poisson schedule fixed before the run; latency from the intended send time; every stage stamped by one host's clock
Because
Closed loop samples a stall once; stamps on one message in one clock make every stage a subtraction.
Problem, options, cost and links
Serves: R2 Open-loop, one clock, no network
Problem
A tail is only as honest as where its clock starts.
Options
Closed loop: send the next order when the response arrivesClosed-loop demonstration cells beside the open-loop onesA Poisson schedule fixed before the run; latency from the intended send time; every stage stamped by one host's clock
Cost
Stamps cost about 0.14 µs, measured in one cell and not netted out; no network hop is measured.
Because (full)
Closed loop is coordinated omission; demonstration cells were cut for build hours, with Tene cited instead. Stamps on one message make each stage a subtraction, in one host's clock. Frames are pre-encoded, so the generator encodes nothing in the window.
Cost (full)
Full stamping added 134–139 ns at the median and 141–166 ns at p99.99 in one calibration cell (exploratory), not netted out. Generator lag includes wire-ring backpressure. No network is measured.

K3Split the hand-off into structure and wait strategy

A 2×2: ArrayBlockingQueue or Disruptor ring, each parking or spinning, same slots and capacity (4,096), at three rates
Because
Only a 2×2 can split the 2011 ring-versus-queue gap into structure and waiting; the queue's lock counts as structure.
Problem, options, cost and links
Serves: R3 One swapped object per experiment
Seen in: F2 Hand-off p99.99 against load: ring or queue, spin or park · F6 EXPLORATORY, POST HOC: when do the queue's G1 pauses reach the p99.99?
Problem
The 2011 Disruptor paper does not say which wait strategy its run used, so its ring-versus-queue gap cannot be split into structure and waiting.
Options
Ring against queue only, the original framingPick E2's hand-off from E1's winnerA 2×2: ArrayBlockingQueue or Disruptor ring, each parking or spinning, same slots and capacity (4,096), at three rates
Cost
The park arms differ in more than structure; one queue tested; spinning costs three cores per host.
Because (full)
Only a 2×2 splits the gap. Structure includes its lock: the queue's lock and wait nodes allocate, and a waiting thread parks, even in the spin arm when two threads collide, so that is measured per thread, not removed. E2 stays at ring + busy-spin whatever E1 shows.
Cost (full)
The park arms differ by construction: a ring + park producer facing a full ring sleeps about 50 µs of timer slack; a queue + park producer is signalled. One queue tested. Spinning keeps three of six cores busy: $0.205/h per host.

K4Sweep durability where per-order sync can keep up

Serves: R3 One swapped object per experiment · R4 Rules fixed before the campaign
Seen in: F1 Who owns the tail, by durability level · F3 Group commit and per-order sync against the page-cache write
Problem
One fdatasync per order caps D3 near one order per sync, so at E1's rates D3 would saturate everywhere.
Options
One rate set for both experimentsMake D3's saturation a hypothesisD0 to D3 at 5,000, 20,000 and 100,000/s; D3's ceiling stated as arithmetic; a recon rule for slow drives
Because
Rates where D3 can stay unsaturated make the durability hypotheses testable; D3's limit is arithmetic, not a hypothesis.
Cost
Drive-specific sizes; D3 reached only 35% of the arithmetic ceiling, unexplained; power loss untested.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
The senior review found one rate set left D3 saturated at every hypothesis rate. By arithmetic, 1 ÷ fio's median put D3's ceiling, an upper bound, near 68,200/s, between 20,000 and 100,000. A pre-written journal makes fdatasync flush data, not allocation metadata.
Cost
Sizes are drive-specific. Saturated D3 forks sustained 23,929–23,979/s, 35% of the arithmetic, and the pipeline's median sync (23.5 µs) sat above fio's 14.7 µs, unexplained. NVMe interrupts land on the journaler's core. Power loss untested.

K5Credit causes by coverage of the excess, against a shifted null

Independent credits for causes covering at least 50% of the excess; a shifted null; a published residual
Because
Overlap is biased by a message's own length; coverage of the excess plus a shifted null removes most of that bias.
Problem, options, cost and links
Serves: R1 Attribute the tail, name the rest · R4 Rules fixed before the campaign
Seen in: F1 Who owns the tail, by durability level
Problem
Slow messages overlap more of everything because they last longer, so overlap alone proves nothing.
Options
Credit a cause whenever it overlaps a tail messageOne exclusive precedence order, used for the tests tooIndependent credits for causes covering at least 50% of the excess; a shifted null; a published residual
Cost
A residual up to about a tenth of the tail, lower-bound nulls for gaps, uncredited handshakes.
Because (full)
The design review found overlap biased by each message's length, messages queued behind an ended stall filed as "queueing", and precedence partly deciding H6. Coverage of the excess removes most length bias, busy periods catch the queued messages, independent credits keep order out of tests. Planted cases were recovered before any real bundle.
Cost (full)
At D0 and D1, 6.66%–10.1% of tail messages stayed residual. Sentinels see gaps only between idle polls, so the gap null is a lower bound. Handshakes are uncredited (their log lines name no thread). One safepoint can also count as a gap.

K6The launch is the unit; intervals over rounds

Launches as the unit: 10 rounds × 28 forks × 3 hosts, contrasts within rounds, intervals over rounds, same-seed replicates
Because
Messages from one JVM are not independent samples; launches and rounds are the honest units.
Problem, options, cost and links
Serves: R5 Intervals over launches · R4 Rules fixed before the campaign
Seen in: F4 Every pre-registered verdict, as it came out · F5 One run's precision against launch-to-launch spread (H4)
Problem
A fork holds millions of messages, so an interval from them is tiny and assumes they are independent.
Options
Intervals over the messages of one long runA percentile bootstrap over the ten roundsResampling forks across roundsLaunches as the unit: 10 rounds × 28 forks × 3 hosts, contrasts within rounds, intervals over rounds, same-seed replicates
Cost
Coarse distribution-free intervals; n = 3 hosts; nothing reported past p99.99.
Because (full)
Messages in one JVM share its JIT decisions, layout and host. Same-seed replicates change only the launch (H4). A round is one host in one stretch of time, paired. With ten blocks a percentile bootstrap of a median under-covers; resampling across rounds breaks the pairing.
Cost (full)
Hosts share each round's seed, so the 30 blocks are not independent, and the intervals (2nd and 9th of ten values, 97.9%) are coarse. H5 is descriptive; generalising rests on n = 3 hosts.

Three rented bare-metal servers, one JVM per run, no network; nothing comparable to kernel-bypass or FPGA systems.

Interview card

30 seconds. I built a deterministic Java matching engine, ran it open-loop as one in-process pipeline on three rented bare-metal servers, and attributed its slowest messages to causes, with rules fixed before the campaign. The lead finding: which hand-off decision sets the tail depends on load. At low load, spinning versus parking matters more than ring versus queue; at 500,000 messages a second the queue's tail goes to milliseconds while the ring's stays in single microseconds. And once an fdatasync is on the response path, waiting for it is the whole tail.
The 2-minute version

In 2011 the Disruptor paper showed a ring beating a queue, but not whether that was the data structure or the consumer spinning instead of sleeping. I built a deterministic Java matching engine, eight order books behind a sequencer and a journal, as one pipeline of five pinned threads, driven open-loop from each message's intended send time. Two experiments: ring or ArrayBlockingQueue crossed with spin or park, and four durability levels from no journal to an fdatasync per order. Six hypotheses pre-registered, 840 JVM launches on three hosts, tail messages attributed against a shifted null with the residual published. Most predictions did not hold, and those are the findings. At 10,000 messages a second waiting mattered more than structure; at 500,000 the queue went to about two milliseconds while the spinning ring stayed at 1.53 µs, and a post hoc count, with arithmetic, puts G1 pauses across the p99.99 rank. With no sync on the path the match stage is the tail; with one, durability wait is all of it, and group commit came out 52 times the page-cache write here, against a predicted under 2. And same-seed JVM launches differed several times more than one run's interval admits.

Hard follow-ups

Your queue's millisecond tail at 500,000/s: is that the queue, or your one-GC-thread collector setup?

Both. The allocation is structural: the queue's lock and wait nodes allocate (a thread that finds the lock held, or an empty queue in the park arm, enqueues a node and parks), while the ring arms' stages allocated nothing. The pause length is partly configuration: one G1 thread on a core shared with the OS and JIT. The breakdown is post hoc (every queue-arm safepoint a G1 allocation pause, medians 2.49 to 2.68 ms). A better-tuned JVM was not tested.

Why ArrayBlockingQueue at all? Does "ring beats queue" still mean anything?

It is the queue the 2011 comparison used, and the question was structure against waiting, with the lock counted as structure. So the claim is ring against ArrayBlockingQueue, one JDK, one collector. No lock-free, non-allocating queue was tested; I would not claim the ring beats queues in general.

Your drives sync in about 15 µs. What does that tell me about my hardware, and what did you test about power loss?

Only the direction transfers. fio's isolated sync median was about 15 µs on drives that report no volatile write cache, but inside the pipeline the median sync took 23.5 µs, for reasons not separated. One published measurement: 1,643 µs on a consumer drive with a write-back cache, 24 µs on an enterprise one. Check the write-cache bit. Power loss was not tested: the crash check kills the JVM, which tests process-crash safety only, and at D1 a pass is timing.

The workload is synthetic, Poisson and thin. Which conclusions move with real order flow?

Not tested; a planned sequel uses an IEX-fitted workload. Arrivals were Poisson on purpose, since bursts would confound the rate factor, and the books are thin, about 264 resting orders over 8 symbols. The matcher's cost depends on how many levels a crossing order touches, so matcher-path numbers depend on book shape.

How much of what you measured is your own instrumentation?

Stamping cost 134–139 ns at the median and 141–166 ns at p99.99 in one calibration cell, exploratory and not netted out, large against a 1.39 µs tail. Logs are synchronous. Sentinels see gaps only between idle polls, handshakes are uncredited, and 6.66%–10.1% of the ungated tail is residual.

How do you know the rules came before the data?

The hypotheses and their thresholds are in the design approved 2026-10-08, before any host was rented. Amendment 1 changed definitions, not hypotheses, before any host ran; laptop runs (not data) prompted it. Reconnaissance's single forks on h1 (not data) had shown D2's p99.99 at 71,416 ns against D1's 1,417 ns and D3 saturating near 23,900/s; H3a (within 2.00×) was kept, and the pre-registration records this. Tag 18:24:42Z on 2026-10-10, first campaign fork 18:28:23Z, no amendment since.

Limits: do not claim more. Three hosts of one server type and one provider, identical journal drives: n = 3. Synthetic workload, thin books, Poisson arrivals, no fills. In-process, no network. One JDK and one collector (G1, one GC thread); one queue. Process crash tested, not power loss. D3's arithmetic is not a measurement (35% reached). Coarse money: three rates, the median launch; a 1.00× tax means invisible to the grid. Attribution floor: the residual, sentinel blind spots, lower-bound nulls, uncredited handshakes; a pause can also count as a gap. Stamp cost and synchronous logs not netted out. The GC breakdown is post hoc.

Recall check

Answer out loud first, then open.

Why does a closed-loop load generator under-report the tail, and where does this pipeline start each message's clock?

It waits for each response, so a stall is sampled once instead of once per message that was due. Here the schedule is fixed first and latency is released minus intended, so a late generator counts against the system.

Why does the gateway fan out to the journaler even at D0, and what contrast does that make clean?

So D0 and D1 differ only in the journal I/O (D0 consumes and discards). On these hosts the write cost nothing visible: 1.38 against 1.39 µs at 20,000/s.

At D2, what does a response wait for, and how is "no response before durable" checked without trusting clocks?

The match and the journaler's durable sequence reaching its own. The output records the durable sequence it saw at release; every message must show one at least its own sequence number.

An ArrayBlockingQueue polled in a spin loop: how can it still park, and why does that matter at 500,000/s?

Its one lock: a contending thread enqueues a node and parks, which allocates. The allocations trigger G1 pauses that stop every thread, and at 500,000/s those delay more messages than the p99.99 rank (post hoc).

Why does a 2.68 ms G1 pause at 10,000/s not show in the queue + park arm's 6.13 µs p99.99?

A p99.99 sees a pause only if the messages it delays outnumber 0.01% of the window: about 27 due during the pause (a floor: the backlog adds more) against a rank of 200 in 2,000,000 messages (arithmetic, not measurement).

Why is "group commit costs 52×" not a property of group commit?

It is this setup at 20,000/s: a tiny ungated tail (1.39 µs) against 72.1 µs. fio measured an isolated sync at about 15 µs on drives with no volatile cache, but inside the pipeline the median sync took 23.5 µs, and a D2 message waits between one and two of them (see How it works). The direction transfers; the size does not.

Why did D3 saturate at 100,000/s, and why was that never a hypothesis?

One fdatasync per order: 1 ÷ fio's median sync (14,656 ns on h1) is about 68,200/s, an upper bound that ignores the write and the hand-offs, and the pipeline sustained about 23,900/s. The arithmetic and reconnaissance both pointed there, so it was a stated prediction, not a hypothesis, and the cell left every test.

A tail message overlaps a safepoint. When is the safepoint credited, and why not on overlap alone?

Only if safepoint time inside its busy period covers at least half its excess over the median. Slow messages overlap more of everything; coverage removes most of that bias, and the shifted null gives chance.

A colleague shows one fork's very tight interval on p99.99 and calls it the answer. What do you say?

That interval assumes independent messages. Same-seed launches spread 5.64 to 8.90 times one run's own error (H4), so honest intervals are over launches or rounds.
