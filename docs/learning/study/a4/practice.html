Practice: placing twenty models on GPUs

Practice for: Dedicate, swap or share: placing twenty small models on GPUs, and a simulator that missed its own test

Try every question before you open its answer. The study page is the reading; this page is the doing.

Objectives

  • Derive 21, 12 and 10 GPUs at the reference point from the hot rule and pairing, and price them ($850.43 a tenant a month for dedicate).
  • Draw one against two vLLM engines on a 24 GB card with GiB labels, and compute each KV ceiling from 114,688 bytes a token.
  • Explain with numbers why equal shares give a thrashing spread pool (hit rate 0.00) and a calm bursty one (0.82, pinned included).
  • Say which claims are measured, simulated or untested, what the gate covered, and why recalibrating the swap time would not pass it.
  • Argue a placement for a new workload from swap time, slowdown, KV ceiling and SLO, and name what you would measure first.

Check yourself first

Three questions the study page prepared you for. Answer out loud, then open.

1What shares a 24 GB card's memory inside one vLLM engine, and which part gets whatever is left?

Show the answer

Weights, runtime and activation memory, and the KV cache. vLLM gives what remains of the engine's memory budget to the KV cache, so shrinking the budget shrinks the KV cache first.

Estimation drills

Do the arithmetic on paper first, then open the worked steps.

Price the reference point from scratch

Bursty arrivals, s = 1.0, 20 models, one GPU of offered load (8.8946 req/s). Hot threshold 0.7, peak factor 5, and a simulated swap pool of 8 GPUs beside the pinned ones. GPU-hour $1.1095, 730 hours a month, 256 output tokens a request. Derive GPUs, dollars per tenant per month, dollars per million output tokens and load per GPU for the three strategies.

Worked answer
  1. Shares: H = sum of 1/k for k = 1 to 20 = 3.5977, so the top share is 1 / 3.5977 = 0.278 and the second is 0.139.
  2. Peak load = share × 1 GPU × 5: 1.39 and 0.695. Only model 1 is hot: ceil(1.39 / 0.7) = ceil(1.985) = 2 pinned GPUs. That leaves 19 tail models.
  3. Dedicate = 2 + 19 = 21. Co-locate floor = 2 + ceil(19 / 2) = 2 + 10 = 12 (9 pairs and 1 single). Swap = 2 + the simulated pool of 8 = 10; its search floor would be 3.
  4. One GPU-month = 1.1095 × 730 = $809.935.
  5. Monthly cost = GPUs × 809.935: $17,008.64, $8,099.35 and $9,719.22. Per tenant (divide by 20): $850.43, $404.97 and $485.96. Dedicate minus swap = 11 × 809.935 = $8,909.29 (8,909.285 exactly; the post prints $8,909.28, rounding down).
  6. Output tokens a month = 8.8946 × 256 × 3,600 × 730 = 5,984 million. Dollars per million: 17,008.64 / 5,984 = $2.84; 8,099.35 / 5,984 = $1.35; 9,719.22 / 5,984 = $1.62.
  7. Load per GPU = 1 / GPUs: 4.8%, 10% and 8.3%.

Answer. 21, 10 and 12 GPUs; $850.43, $404.97 and $485.96 per tenant per month; $2.84, $1.35 and $1.62 per million output tokens; 4.8%, 10% and 8.3% load. Simulated and unvalidated; dollars assume every GPU runs all month.

KV ceilings of one engine and of two, from the card

Card 23.52 GiB, 114,688 bytes of KV per token (Qwen3-1.7B), requests of 2,048 tokens. A lone engine at 0.92 logged 168,464 KV tokens. Find its fixed cost outside the KV cache, predict a split engine's KV at 0.45 and compare with the logged 64,976 (warm) and 55,104 (compiled), then give the three request ceilings.

Worked answer
  1. Per token: 2 (K and V) × 28 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 head dimension × 2 bytes = 114,688 B.
  2. Lone budget = 0.92 × 23.52 = 21.64 GiB. Its KV = 168,464 × 114,688 B / 2^30 = 17.99 GiB. Fixed cost outside the KV cache = 21.64 − 17.99 = 3.64 GiB (weights among it).
  3. Split budget = 0.45 × 23.52 = 10.58 GiB. Same fixed cost: KV = 10.58 − 3.64 = 6.94 GiB, about 64,970 tokens. The warm engine logged 64,976, so the fixed-cost model is right to within about 10 tokens.
  4. The registered 55,104 tokens are 5.89 GiB: the compiled engine's reading, pinned for both (6,319,767,552 bytes, 3,444 blocks of 16 tokens). Each engine gives up about 1.05 GiB against the warm reading.
  5. Ratios: budget 0.92 / 0.45 = 2.04; KV 168,464 / 64,976 = 2.6 (the fixed cost); 168,464 / 55,104 = 3.1; the pin 64,976 / 55,104 = 1.18.
  6. Ceilings at 2,048 tokens: 168,464 / 2,048 = 82; 64,976 / 2,048 = 31; 55,104 / 2,048 = 26 (26 to 28 were seen running).

Answer. Fixed cost about 3.64 GiB; predicted 64,970 warm split tokens (logged 64,976); pinned 55,104; ceilings 82, 31 and 26. A fraction appears to budget one engine rather than partition the card (inferred); the pin is what was tried and it fit.

Deep-dive drill

Ordered easy to hard. Answer out loud, open the model answer, then the red-flag answer: the tempting reply that sounds right and is not.

Drill 1 of 12

Why can't you compare dedicate, swap and co-locate at the same number of GPUs?

Model answer

At equal counts they cost the same by construction. The variable is the smallest fleet each needs to meet the SLO, found by one shared rule: hot models pinned alike, then the smallest fleet whose every popularity decile meets the 75.6 s p99. Cost follows from that count. Analogy: size each cache tier to its own hit-rate target, not to a shared node count.

Red-flag answer

"Compare latency at 12 GPUs each." That lets the fixed count decide the winner.

Drill 2 of 12

Why is the top model 'hot' in the bursty regime at s = 1.0 but never in the spread regime, even at s = 2.0?

Model answer

The rule sizes for peak load: share × 1 GPU × 5 when bursty (a model is ON a fifth of the time), × 1 when spread. At s = 1.0 the top share is 0.278, so 1.39 GPU while ON: over 0.7, ceil(1.39 / 0.7) = ceil(1.985) = 2 pinned GPUs. Spread at s = 2.0 gives the top model 0.627 of a GPU (1 / 1.5962), under 0.7, so none is hot.

Red-flag answer

"Hot means a high share." That misses the peak factor, which only the bursty regime has.

Drill 3 of 12

Why can dedicating at best tie, and what is the finding then?

Model answer

Swap's search ends with a pool GPU per tail model and co-locate's with every pair split. Both are dedicate's placement at dedicate's size, so the search stops there and dedicate can tie but never win. 'Never cheapest' is construction plus margin. The simulated margin, about twice the cheapest, is the finding.

Red-flag answer

"Dedicating lost because it wastes GPUs." That states the result without the mechanism and hides that part of it is by construction.

Drill 4 of 12

Co-locate sat exactly at its pairing floor at every evaluable point. What did the simulator add?

Model answer

Its counts are arithmetic: pinned GPUs plus half the rest, paired (10 spread; 12, 14, 15, 16 bursty). The simulator only showed that the fully paired fleet meets the 75.6 s SLO, at a p99 of about 2 to 6 s. The uncertainty is whether pairing passes under real interference, not how many GPUs. The exploratory x4 run is where co-locate left its floor.

Red-flag answer

Presenting 12 as a fine-grained optimisation result.

Drill 5 of 12

Halving each engine's memory share cut KV capacity 3.1 times. Walk me through the memory.

Model answer

Card 23.52 GiB. A lone engine at 0.92 has 21.64 GiB; its 168,464 KV tokens × 114,688 B are 17.99 GiB, so about 3.64 GiB sits outside the KV cache. A split engine at 0.45 has 10.58 GiB, leaving 6.94 GiB: about 64,970 tokens, and a warm engine logged 64,976. That is 2.6 times. The registered 55,104 is the compiled engine's reading, pinned for both so two warm engines fit: the other 1.18.

Red-flag answer

"KV halves because memory halves", or "3.1 times because of the weights" with no numbers.

Drill 6 of 12

Why did two warm engines at 0.45 each run out of memory when 0.45 + 0.45 leaves room?

Model answer

A fraction appears to budget one engine rather than partition the card (inferred; the 1.26 GiB over budget was not itemised). Both engines hit the compile cache, so each took about 64,900 KV tokens. The first held 11.84 GiB against a 10.58 GiB budget, the second 11.16, the worker 0.5: 15 MiB free when the second's graph capture asked for 20 MiB. Pinning KV to 55,104 tokens (6,319,767,552 bytes) gave about 1 GiB back each.

Red-flag answer

"The two budgets add to 90%, so it fits." Memory outside the budgets (likely contexts and graphs; not measured) collides.

Drill 7 of 12

Why does a busy neighbour cost about 2.3 times even with one request in flight, and what would you measure?

Model answer

Measured: 2.95 s against 1.27 s at own concurrency 1 with a neighbour at 16, 2.0 to 2.4 times at every load. The mechanism was not measured. Hypothesis only: the engines contend for one GPU's compute and bandwidth, a share of the device that does not depend on your own load; the KV pin keeps their memory apart. Analogy: two pods with no CPU limits on one node. Next: profile both processes at own 1 with neighbour 0, 1 to 15, 16.

Red-flag answer

"They compete for KV memory" (KV capacity is pinned and separate; bandwidth is another matter), or stating the contention story as measured.

Drill 8 of 12

Same per-model shares, yet swap wins when bursty and loses when spread. Show it with numbers.

Model answer

Simulated, s = 1.0. Spread, pool 13: hit rate 0.00, 1,094.8 swaps an hour, a swap start every 42.7 s per pool GPU against a 37.8 s median swap. Bursty, pool 8: hit 0.82 over all arrivals, pinned model included (about 0.75 for the pool alone), 96.5 an hour, every 298.5 s. The pool schedules again right after each swap, so a waiting model evicts the LRU serving GPU at once; a 120 s burst spreads one swap over many requests. Analogy: cache thrash.

Red-flag answer

"Bursty is lower load." Shares and load are identical by construction; only timing differs.

Drill 9 of 12

Your SLO is twice the swap time. Defend it, then attack it.

Model answer

Defend: a ranking-blind screen chose it before the measurements, counting how many strategies a point tells apart, never which is cheaper; the placeholder 4 s SLO was shorter than one swap. Attack: two swaps of budget absorb a swap, which favours swapping. Even the simulated spread pool at s = 1.0, with hit rate 0.0, met it (worst decile 74.3 s against 75.6). Only one SLO was swept.

Red-flag answer

"The SLO was picked to make swap win." It was picked blind, but it does favour swap, and you should say so.

Drill 10 of 12

Charged 37.8 s per swap the simulator is too slow; charged about 25 s it is too fast. What does that imply?

Model answer

No fixed stall fits. Charged 38 s, 2 of 13 bins agree (largest miss 69.2 s; the gate's own run at 37.8 s had 3 of 13 and 67.8 s, so a rounded charge moves a bin); at 28 s, 6; at 25 s, 2 (10.4 s, too fast); each repeat judged on its own swap time still misses 12 of 13. The model has no term for what a swap costs the requests around it (not measured). A calibrated re-run would not pass; measure that cost on one pod.

Red-flag answer

"Calibrate the swap time and re-run the gate." That is a forking path, and it still fails.

Drill 11 of 12

What would a passing gate have bought you here, and what would it still not cover?

Model answer

It would cover one GPU, three tenants and swapping only, at 180 s bursts, duty 0.25 and 0.3 of a GPU. It would not cover LRU victim choice across GPUs, fleets of 9 to 25 GPUs, the spread regime, the sweep's 120 s bursts at load 1.0, or co-location, which had two held-out cells and one missed. A credible fleet claim needs a multi-GPU replay per strategy.

Red-flag answer

"If the gate passed, the GPU counts are validated."

Drill 12 of 12

Spread arrivals made the simulated swap pool thrash. Name mitigations a production pool could add, and how you would check them.

Model answer

Not tested here: the simulator evicts at once, with no hysteresis. Candidates from cache practice: a minimum residency time before a just-loaded model may be evicted; evict only when a waiter has waited past a threshold; for the cold tail a cheaper switch such as sleep mode (5.0 s, host memory unmeasured). Add the policy to the simulator, compare hit rate and swaps per hour, then replay. Each trades the evicted model's wait against the waiter's.

Red-flag answer

Claiming a mitigation works because it sounds standard, or "just add GPUs" without the dollar cost.

Design scenario

You host 50 distinct small models (1 to 4B parameters) for business tenants on 24 GB GPUs. Traffic is office-hours bursty per tenant, total load about 3 GPUs at peak, and the contract SLO is p99 10 s per tenant. Design the placement, and say what you would measure before you commit.

Outline of a strong answer
  1. Reframe: 50 models against about 3 GPUs of load is a residency problem. Price each way of keeping models resident (a GPU each, a swap pool, pairs) as this study did, and size every option by one rule, per tenant.
  2. The SLO decides first: 10 s is shorter than the 37.8 s process swap measured here (about 25 s on another pod). A pool of process swaps cannot serve a cold request in time; only a switch like sleep mode (5.0 s) could, if 5 s plus service fits, and its host memory is unmeasured.
  3. Tiers (a design, not tested here): pin hot tenants by peak load, not average; co-locate pairs only after checking each size's KV ceiling (the 4B pair left 9,456 and 9,520 tokens against a 16,384 floor); keep the cold tail asleep or resident.
  4. Interference budget: co-locating won here on headroom, a few seconds against 75.6 s. Against 10 s a 2.0 to 2.4 times slowdown may not fit. That is an inference to measure for the real pairs, including neighbours at 1 to 15.
  5. Account per tenant (decile or tenant p99), never the aggregate, which hid the two hottest deciles here.
  6. Anti-thrash: the simulated pool evicted at once. Any production policy (minimum residency, wait threshold) must be added to the model and checked, not assumed.
  7. Measure first: wake and sleep latency under load, host memory per sleeping model, interference for the real pairs, KV ceilings at the real request shapes, and what a swap or wake costs the requests around it, the term this study lacked.
  8. Validate: a pre-registered multi-GPU replay per strategy, then a shadow of production traffic. Publish misses.
Pitfalls to check your answer against
  • Copying 'co-locate when spread' without noticing that the SLO is far tighter than 75.6 s.
  • Ignoring host memory when proposing sleep mode, which this study never priced.
  • Sizing on the aggregate p99, or hot models on average load.
  • Presenting a simulated GPU count as a finding after a failed gate.

Lab

Runs on your machine

Goal. See thrash as a measurable state and see where the sized fleets come from, using the cached sweep and the placement code, with no GPU and no paid call.

CPU only, minutes, read-only. Label every output exploratory and keep it out of data/a4/. A follow-up that needs a GPU (profiling two engines on one card) is not part of this lab and would be a paid run.

Setup

  • Use .venv/bin/python with PYTHONPATH=. and never uv run: it re-syncs the venv and has broken byte-compared figure tests.
  • Read-only input: build/a4-sweep/evaluations-5f2ab57d0f9f2e86.json (30 repetitions per point). If it is missing, stop: rebuilding it is hours of CPU.
  • Write scripts and outputs outside data/a4/, in a scratch folder.

Steps

  1. In a Python session: from placement.evaluate import load_evaluations; ev = load_evaluations(Path(the cache file)). Pick the PointEvaluation whose point.regime is spread, then bursty, with point.s == 1.0.
  2. For ev.outcomes['swap'] (one ConfigOutcome per pool size M, ascending) print m, sum(hits) / sum(requests) and the mean of window_swaps per repetition. Plot hit rate against M for both regimes.
  3. Re-size with placement.sizing.sized_fleet(ev, list(range(30)), slo) at slo = 75.55 (2 × the 37.78 s median swap) and check it returns the registered 20, 13, 10 (spread) and 21, 10, 12 (bursty).
  4. Repeat with slo = 1.0, 1.5 and 3.0 times 37.78 s. Record each strategy's M.
  5. Hot rule: from placement.traffic import zipf_shares; from placement.fleet import hot_allocation, family. With shares = zipf_shares(20, 1.0), call hot = hot_allocation(shares, 1.0, 0.7, 5.0), then read len(p.gpus) for each p in family(strategy, shares, hot). Repeat with 0.69.

Record

  • Hit rate and mean swaps per repetition at each pool size, both regimes
  • Sized M per strategy at each SLO multiple
  • Pinned GPUs and the dedicate and co-locate sizes at hot fraction 0.7 and 0.69
Expected result and what it shows

Expected. Predictions to check, not facts. Step 2: spread hit rate near 0 until the pool nears the number of tail models, bursty hit rate from the pinned share (0.28) to 0.82 at M = 10 (pool 8; 0.65 at M = 8). Step 3 reproduces the registered sizes. Step 4: swap's M should rise as the multiple falls and fall as it rises, while co-locate and dedicate stay put. Step 5: pinned GPUs 2 at 0.7 and 5 at 0.69; dedicate 21 then 23, swap's ladder 3 to 21 then 6 to 23, co-locate's 12 to 21 then 14 to 23.

Shows. Which counts are arithmetic and which come from the SLO search, how fragile swap's win is to the SLO choice, and thrash as a number (hit rate).
