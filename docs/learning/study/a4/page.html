Study page · about 45 min

Dedicate, swap or share: placing twenty small models on GPUs, and a simulator that missed its own test

Read first: LLM serving on a GPU · How these experiments earn trust

Twenty small models on rented RTX 4090s: dedicate a GPU to each, swap models through a shared pool, or co-locate two per GPU? Which is cheapest as popularity skew and arrival timing change? The answer comes from a simulator that failed its pre-registered validation gate.

Answer first

I asked how to place twenty small LLMs on rented GPUs: give each its own GPU, swap models in and out of a shared pool, or pair them on one card. I measured what the hardware charges for a swap and for a busy neighbour, and fed that into a fleet simulator. Its answer: how traffic arrives picks the winner, not how skewed it is. Pair models when requests are spread out, swap when they come in bursts; dedicating was never cheapest. But the simulator failed its own pre-registered test, 10 of 13 bins, so I publish the GPU counts as unvalidated.

Need to know first

C1Zipf skew, and hot models

The k-th most popular of 20 models gets a share proportional to 1 / k to the power s. At s = 2.0 the hottest model carries about 62.7% (a scope-amendment estimate). Total load: one GPU's measured throughput, 8.89 requests per second. A model above 0.7 of a GPU is hot and gets pinned GPUs in every strategy.
Used by: D2 One sizing rule for every strategy, judged per decile · D3 Two arrival regimes with the same per-model shares

C2Spread and bursty arrivals

Same long-run share per model, different timing. Spread: random (Poisson) arrivals, each labelled independently. Bursty: each model is ON in bursts of about 120 s, a fifth of the time. In a burst its load is five times its average, so bursty sizing uses that ON-period load.
Used by: D3 Two arrival regimes with the same per-model shares

C3A swap, and the LRU pool

Stop one vLLM engine, wait for its memory to free, start the next model's engine until it answers health: median 37.8 s over 16 warm swaps, compile cache hit. The swap strategy's pool evicts the least recently used model. Sleep mode (5.0 s) was measured, never simulated.
Used by: D1 A new fleet simulator, fed three measured primitives · D5 Load and SLO from a ranking-blind screen · D6 Hold the simulator to a one-GPU replay; publish the miss

C4Co-location and the KV split

Two engines on one card, 45% of its memory each. The KV cache falls from 168,464 tokens alone to 55,104 (pinned): 82 requests of 2,048 tokens at once, against 26. It shrinks 3.1 times while the memory share shrinks 2.0 times, because the weights and graph buffers come out of the smaller budget first.
Used by: D1 A new fleet simulator, fed three measured primitives · D4 A small model, picked by a go/no-go, with KV that binds

C5The SLO, per popularity decile

SLO: p99 at most 75.6 s, twice the median swap. A decile is 2 of the 20 models by popularity. Its p99 is taken in each simulated run, then the median over 30 runs. A fleet size passes only if all ten deciles meet the SLO.
Used by: D2 One sizing rule for every strategy, judged per decile · D5 Load and SLO from a ranking-blind screen

C6The validation gate, as used here

Three tenants on one GPU, a 900 s bursty trace of 1,562 requests with real swaps, replayed three times. Per 30 s bin, the simulator's median latency must fall inside the three repeats' band. Pass: at least 10 judged bins, at most half missing, swap count within the real range ±1. Co-location gets only two held-out interference cells.
Used by: D6 Hold the simulator to a one-GPU replay; publish the miss

Requirements that drove everything

R1One sizing rule for all three

Size every strategy by the same rule: hot models pinned the same way, then the smallest fleet that meets the SLO.
If only some strategies may give a hot model extra GPUs, the rule, not the measurement, decides the high-skew result (scope amendment §7).
Answered by: D2 One sizing rule for every strategy, judged per decile · D5 Load and SLO from a ranking-blind screen

R2Every tenant meets the SLO

Hold the latency target for every popularity decile of models, not only for all requests together.
Popular models send most requests, so an aggregate p99 can pass while some tenants fail (design §8).
Answered by: D2 One sizing rule for every strategy, judged per decile

R3Skew and arrival timing, apart

Sweep popularity skew at two arrival regimes that give each model the same long-run share.
Swapping with LRU follows whether a model's requests come together in time, not how often they come; skew alone misses that (design §7).
Answered by: D3 Two arrival regimes with the same per-model shares

R4Measure primitives, simulate fleets

Measure on the GPU what a swap, a co-located neighbour and a memory split cost; simulate only traffic, fleet dynamics and queueing.
They are the design's only hardware contacts; interference "cannot be reasoned to" (design, module 2).
Answered by: D1 A new fleet simulator, fed three measured primitives · D4 A small model, picked by a go/no-go, with KV that binds

R5Rules first; trust only after a gate

Fix every rule before the data it governs, with dated amendments; trust the simulator only if it reproduces a real replay, and publish a miss.
Fleet numbers are a model's; only a test fixed in advance separates a finding from model error (a failed gate is a result).
Answered by: D4 A small model, picked by a go/no-go, with KV that binds · D5 Load and SLO from a ranking-blind screen · D6 Hold the simulator to a one-GPU replay; publish the miss

R6Answer in GPUs and dollars

State the result as a decision rule in GPUs and monthly dollars, with every conversion assumption published.
The portfolio requires money too, and readers must be able to substitute their own rate.
Answered by: D2 One sizing rule for every strategy, judged per decile

One swap inside the simulator, in order

This path, on one GPU, is what the gate replayed. The swap is a fixed stall of the GPU; nothing models what a swap costs the requests around it, the term the diagnosis says is missing.

  • 1 Request for model x — x is on no GPU; it waits in its queue
  • 2 Pick the LRU victim — the pool GPU used longest ago
  • 3 Victim drains — admits nothing; in-flight finish
  • 4 Swap starts key step — GPU stalls a drawn time, median 37.8 s
  • 5 Two queues wait — model x, and the evicted model
  • 6 Swap done — x resident; its queue is dispatched
  • 7 Service time — solo curve at dispatch, then frozen
  • 8 Latency recorded — arrival to done; every request served
  1. 1 Request for model x → 2 Pick the LRU victim: no swap pending
  2. 2 Pick the LRU victim → 3 Victim drains: victim chosen
  3. 3 Victim drains → 4 Swap starts: last one done
  4. 4 Swap starts → 5 Two queues wait: meanwhile
  5. 5 Two queues wait → 6 Swap done: stall ends
  6. 6 Swap done → 7 Service time: dispatch
  7. 7 Service time → 8 Latency recorded: done

Core decisions

D1A new fleet simulator, fed three measured primitives

Serves: R4 Measure primitives, simulate fleets
Seen in: F3 Measured: a busy neighbour roughly doubles latency at every load · F4 Measured: where a 37.8 s swap goes · F6 EXPLORATORY, SIMULATED: how much interference co-locating can absorb
Problem
The question is about fleets of 10 to 25 GPUs and 20 models; the August design planned to extend artifact 2's simulator.
Options
Extend artifact 2's simulator with a model dimension (the August design)Assume co-located models each get half the GPUA new loop with GPU identity: route by residency, swap by LRU, serve every request; swap, interference and KV split measured
Because
Artifact 2's loop has no GPU identity, and placement is which GPU holds which model; the half-a-GPU guess had to be measured.
Cost
A new loop to build; service time frozen at dispatch; neighbour load 1 to 15 interpolated, not measured.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
Artifact 2's loop has no replica identity, and placement is exactly which GPU holds which model; changing it would also change artifact 2's code mid-experiment. The half-the-GPU guess is "almost certainly wrong in both directions" (design), so it was measured. Every request is served, because dropping unfinished ones would drop the cold-tail requests stuck behind swaps.
Cost
A new loop to build. Service time is frozen at dispatch, capacity is the top measured concurrency, and neighbour load between 0 and 16 is interpolated, not measured.

D2One sizing rule for every strategy, judged per decile

Serves: R1 One sizing rule for all three · R2 Every tenant meets the SLO · R6 Answer in GPUs and dollars
Seen in: F1 UNVALIDATED: GPUs each strategy needs, by skew and regime · F2 UNVALIDATED: sizing on the aggregate p99 hides the hottest tenants
Problem
At equal GPU counts the strategies cost the same, so each needs its own fleet size, by a rule that cannot decide the result by itself.
Options
Compare the strategies at one fixed number of GPUsLet only some strategies replicate hot modelsSize on the aggregate p99 of all requestsIn the bursty regime, let every strategy search up to a common capOne hot-model rule for all three; the smallest fleet whose every decile meets the SLO; bursty sizing on ON-period load
Because
Hot models are pinned alike in all three, and a fleet passes only if every decile meets the SLO: the aggregate hides the hottest tenants.
Cost
Swap needs a GPU more; dedicating can at best tie by construction; bursty s = 0.6 is not evaluable.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
Hot models get pinned GPUs in every strategy, so strategies differ only in how the rest are placed. The aggregate p99 is mostly popular models' requests: an aggregate rule gives swap 9 GPUs at the reference point while the two hottest deciles sit at 87.7 and 77.2 s. On average load, a placeholder sweep left every bursty strategy dominated; the owner chose ON-period load.
Cost
Swap needs one GPU more at the reference point. Swap and co-locate search only up to dedicate's size, where their layouts become dedicate's, so dedicating can at best tie unless both others miss below it. Bursty s = 0.6 is not evaluable: a decile had too few requests for a p99.

D3Two arrival regimes with the same per-model shares

Serves: R3 Skew and arrival timing, apart
Seen in: F1 UNVALIDATED: GPUs each strategy needs, by skew and regime
Problem
Skew says how often each model is asked for, not whether its requests come together. Swapping by LRU depends on the second.
Options
Sweep Zipf skew aloneAt each skew, a spread regime (independent arrivals) and a bursty one (ON about a fifth of the time, 120 s bursts), with identical long-run shares
Because
LRU swapping follows whether a model's requests cluster in time, not how often they come, so skew alone would miss the mechanism.
Cost
Only two regimes: the data cannot place the boundary between them.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
Both generators take the same shares, so only clustering in time differs; skew alone "would earn the correct objection that the analysis ignores locality" (design §7). The simulator put the boundary between the regimes, with no crossover along skew in either.
Cost
Only two regimes were built, so the data cannot say where between them the winner changes. The gate's trace used a third shape (180 s bursts at duty 0.25).

D4A small model, picked by a go/no-go, with KV that binds

Serves: R4 Measure primitives, simulate fleets · R5 Rules first; trust only after a gate
Seen in: F3 Measured: a busy neighbour roughly doubles latency at every load
Problem
Two of artifact 1's 8B models (about 16 GB each) do not fit a 24 GB card, and at the earlier short request the KV cache never fills.
Options
Artifact 1's Qwen3-8BA different card for the co-location armKeep the inherited short requestQwen3-4B, falling back to Qwen3-1.7B, by a go/no-go fixed before reconnaissance; a request long enough that the split's KV binds
Because
Two 8B models do not fit one card, and a short request never fills the KV cache; a go/no-go fixed in advance picked the size.
Cost
A small-model result, and the pair's KV had to be pinned by an amendment to fit at all.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
A different card would confound strategy with hardware. Pass: both engines healthy at 45% of memory, each with at least 16,384 KV tokens. The 4B pair left 9,456 and 9,520; the 1.7B pair passed. The shape rule chose 1,792 input and 256 output tokens: 26 requests fit a split engine, 82 a lone one.
Cost
A small-model result (3.8 GiB of weights); swap and co-location costs scale with size. Two warm engines did not fit at 45% each, so their KV was pinned to 55,104 tokens by a signed amendment.

D5Load and SLO from a ranking-blind screen

Serves: R5 Rules first; trust only after a gate · R1 One sizing rule for all three
Problem
On placeholder inputs both regimes came out degenerate: the 4 s SLO was shorter than one swap. Load and SLO decide whether the strategies can differ at all.
Options
Keep the placeholder design point (four GPUs of load, a 4 s SLO)A screen over 3 loads × 3 SLO multiples of the swap median that scores how many strategies each grid point tells apart, never which is cheaper
Because
Counting how many strategies a point tells apart cannot favour one; the placeholder SLO was shorter than a swap.
Cost
Twice a swap favours swapping, and the measured sweep ran at that one SLO.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
A count of distinct fleet sizes cannot favour a strategy. Ties went to the earlier candidate: one GPU of load and twice the swap median, 75.6 s with the measured swaps. The screen's data was committed before the values it set.
Cost
Twice a swap absorbs a swap, which favours swapping: at the reference point swap delivers about 45 s p99, the others 4 to 5 s. The measured sweep ran at this one SLO, and the screen ran on placeholder engines.

D6Hold the simulator to a one-GPU replay; publish the miss

Serves: R5 Rules first; trust only after a gate
Seen in: F5 The gate: the simulator against three real replays
Problem
Fleet numbers mean something only if the simulator reproduces a real system; the design scaled artifact 2's gate down to one GPU.
Options
Trust the simulator because its inputs are measuredDraw each predicted swap from the measured distributionAfter the failure, re-validate with the swap time calibratedThree real replays of one 900 s bursty trace; predict with the median measured swap; publish whatever the verdict
Because
Three real repeats set the tolerance and a fixed swap keeps the prediction deterministic; the verdict is published either way.
Cost
Failed, so GPU counts are unvalidated; co-location was never replayed and one of two held-out cells missed.
Full reasoning and cost
Because
A model cannot be held tighter than three real repeats' spread, and a fixed swap time keeps the prediction deterministic. It failed: 10 of 13 judged bins outside, always too slow. Recalibrating would not rescue it: charged each repeat's own swaps, 12 of 13 still miss, the other way.
Cost
No fleet size is validated: the gate tested one GPU, three tenants and swapping only, at a burst shape and load the sweep did not use; co-location had two held-out cells, one missed. The diagnosis is exploratory.

Interview card

30 seconds. I asked how to place twenty small LLMs on rented GPUs: give each its own GPU, swap models in and out of a shared pool, or pair them on one card. I measured what the hardware charges for a swap and for a busy neighbour, and fed that into a fleet simulator. Its answer: how traffic arrives picks the winner, not how skewed it is. Pair models when requests are spread out, swap when they come in bursts; dedicating was never cheapest. But the simulator failed its own pre-registered test, 10 of 13 bins, so I publish the GPU counts as unvalidated.
The 2-minute version

Serving many models means deciding placement. For 20 small models on RTX 4090s I compared dedicating a GPU each, swapping through a shared LRU pool, and co-locating two per GPU, across Zipf skew and two arrival patterns with the same per-model shares, spread and bursty. On the GPU I measured a swap (median 37.8 s, mostly process start), a busy neighbour (2.0 to 2.4 times slower) and the memory split (KV cache 168,464 down to 55,104 tokens). A new simulator sized each strategy to the smallest fleet where every popularity decile meets a 75.6 s p99, because aggregate sizing let the hottest tenants breach. Result: co-locate when spread, swap when bursty, dedicating about twice the cheapest. But my pre-registered gate, three real replays on one GPU, failed: 10 of 13 bins outside the band. Afterwards I found the simulator charged 37.8 s per swap where the replay pod took about 25 s, and even at that 12 of 13 still missed: it lacks a term for what a swap costs nearby requests. So the ordering is wide and the counts are unvalidated. The paid runs cost $25.23.

Hard follow-ups

Why trust a simulator that failed its gate?

For GPU counts, I don't: the gate failed at its one point (one GPU, three tenants, swapping only), the fleets are 10 to 25 GPUs, and co-location had two held-out cells, one missed. The ordering is wide, about twice, but I did not test whether the model's error leaves it unchanged. The measured swap, slowdown and KV split stand.

Why is dedicating never cheapest?

Partly by construction: swap and co-locate search only up to dedicate's size, where their layouts become dedicate's, so dedicating can at best tie (scope amendment §7; the post does not say this). The finding is the margin: co-locate's 3 to 6 s latency met the 75.6 s SLO at half of dedicate's 20 GPUs.

What would change the winner?

Arrival timing (only two regimes built); the SLO (twice a swap favours swapping, and the sweep ran at one SLO); interference (exploratory: four times the penalty loses co-location the bursty regime, eight the spread one); model size (a 1.7B result).

Why did the gate fail?

Exploratory, after the verdict. A host up to 30% faster does not close it. The gate charged 37.8 s per swap; replay swaps took about 25 s. Charged that, the simulator is too fast and 12 of 13 bins still miss: it has no term for what a swap costs nearby requests.

You switched models and changed rules mid-campaign. Isn't that moving the goalposts?

The 1.7B switch was a branch committed before reconnaissance. The KV pin came after the first co-located runs crashed, before any valid co-located data, confirmed by a probe and signed; the validity and ramp amendments came before the cells were analysed. All dated in git; the re-runs cost 7.45 unplanned GPU-hours.

What would you do next?

Measure what a swap costs the requests around it, on one pod with the replay's own requests: the missing term. A recalibrated re-run would not pass. Co-location needs its own replay.

Limits: do not claim more. Every GPU count, dollar figure and the decision rule are from a simulator that failed its gate (10 of 13 bins), at one GPU, three tenants and swapping only, with a burst shape and load the sweep did not use; co-location had two held-out cells, one missed, and no replay. Whether the model's error leaves the ordering unchanged was not tested. Qwen3-1.7B, not the registered 4B; one size and request shape; LRU only; one GPU class and datacenter; warm swaps only; swap time varies about 50% by pod; one SLO, twice a swap, which favours swapping; two arrival regimes. Sleep mode measured, never simulated. The diagnosis and the interference sensitivity are exploratory. The post is an unpublished draft.

Recall check

Answer out loud first, then open.

Why can the strategies not be compared at the same number of GPUs?

At equal counts they cost the same by construction. Each is sized to its smallest fleet meeting the SLO, and cost follows from that count.

Spread and bursty traffic give each model the same share. What differs, and what does it change?

Timing. In a burst one swap-in serves many requests; scattered, a model is loaded, evicted and loaded again (design module 4). Simulated: swap cheapest when bursty, co-locate when spread.

At the reference point, what does sizing on the aggregate p99 get wrong?

It picks 9 GPUs for swap, and the two hottest deciles breach (87.7 and 77.2 s against 75.6 s); per-decile sizing needs 10. R2.

Which cold-start stages does a swap skip, and what does it still pay?

It skips platform scheduling and compiling (a cache hit). It re-pays process start, imports and the health check, 27.8 of 37.8 s.

Splitting memory halves each engine's share. Why does its KV cache shrink 3.1 times?

Weights and graph buffers are fixed and come out of the smaller budget first: 168,464 to 55,104 tokens, 82 to 26 requests.

A busy neighbour makes a model about twice as slow. Why does co-locating still halve the fleet?

SLO headroom, not low load: 3 to 6 s against 75.6 s. In the exploratory run it held at the worst reading of the unmeasured range.

Why can dedicating at best tie under this sizing rule?

Swap and co-locate search only up to dedicate's size, where their layouts become dedicate's; dedicating wins only if both miss below it. The margin is the finding.

The gate charged 37.8 s per swap; the replay pod's swaps took about 25 s. Why does charging 25 s not rescue the gate?

Then it predicts faster than real and 12 of 13 bins still miss: no fixed swap cost matches, because the model has no term for a swap's effect on nearby requests.

What did the gate test, and what did it never test?

One GPU, three tenants, swapping, 180 s bursts at 0.3 of a GPU. Never co-location (two held-out cells), never a multi-GPU fleet, never the swept 120 s bursts.

Which requirement breaks if the GPU counts are presented as findings, and what may be said instead?

R5. The ordering may be called wide but unvalidated; the measured primitives stand.
