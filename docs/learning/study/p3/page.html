Delivery guarantees and duplicate payments

Used by: a6

ConceptsViewsRecallSources

The ideas

At-least-once, and exactly-once effect

At-least-once: a message may repeat, never be lost. Exactly-once delivery over a network that drops acknowledgements is impossible; systems give exactly-once effect: repeats made harmless.
Like
A courier who redelivers whenever unsure you signed: you never miss a parcel, sometimes you get two.
Matters
A6's consumers are all at-least-once; dual-write producers can also lose. Its stacks, N (naive), K (Kafka transactions), I (key at the sink) and O+I (outbox plus key), differ in where, and whether, repeats are made harmless.

Offsets, committed after the work

A consumer's progress in a partition is an offset: the next position to read. A6's consumers commit it only after their Postgres commit, so a crash in between means the batch is redelivered from the old offset.
Like
A bookmark moved only after you finish the chapter: fall asleep first and you reread the chapter.
Matters
That gap is the commit window. Cell F4-con-t kills a consumer exactly there; on N it is the detection gate's planted duplicate.

Idempotent producer versus transactions

An idempotent producer lets the broker drop a retried batch it already wrote, within one producer session; a restarted process or a relay re-sending is new to it. A Kafka transaction commits Kafka writes and consumer offsets atomically; Postgres is not a participant.
Matters
So K can still apply a batch twice: die between its Postgres commit and commitTransaction, and the batch is redelivered while the Postgres commit stands. A6's H1 tests this.

Consumer groups, rebalances, zombies

A group splits partitions among live members. One silent past session.timeout.ms loses them and a new generation starts; an offset commit from an old generation is rejected: generation fencing. A zombie, paused and woken with a batch in hand, can still write outside Kafka.
Like
A worker dozes off holding a task; it is reassigned. He wakes and finishes it anyway, and only his timesheet is rejected.
Matters
Cell F5 makes one. In reconnaissance on N it wrote its batch to Postgres, then failed its offset commit, in all 17 F5 trials. On K, epoch fencing does not apply: each instance has its own transactional.id.

The dual write, and the outbox

Commit the database, then send to Kafka: two commits in two systems. A crash or send timeout between them leaves a recorded payment never published. The outbox writes the payment and an 'event to publish' row in one transaction; a relay (Debezium, reading the database log) publishes it later, at least once.
Like
Diary, then a text to a friend: if the phone dies in between, the friend never hears. The outbox writes the text in the diary; a courier sends it, maybe twice.
Matters
N, K and I dual-write; cell F4-svc kills the service right after its source commit. O+I's outbox moves duplicates downstream rather than removing them, so it still needs the key.

Idempotency key at the sink

A unique payment_id, assigned at the origin, inserted into a processed table in the same transaction as the balance update: a repeat conflicts and is skipped. It breaks if the key is made downstream, or check and write are separate transactions.
Like
A ticket barcode printed at purchase and scanned at the gate: a second scan is refused. A barcode printed at the gate would defeat it.
Matters
Why not dedupe by Kafka offset? A relay's re-send lands at a new offset; only a key carried from the origin catches it. I and O+I bet on this key.

Checking a history: ok, fail, info

Jepsen-style, record each request as ok, fail (here: provably never reached the service) or info (a timeout or 5xx: may or may not have happened). With unique IDs and commuting increments, counting each ID in source, topic and apply log is a complete check.
Like
A payment page that timed out: the money may or may not have gone; only the statement says.
Matters
A6's source database is the authority and resolves every info. The apply log keeps each apply's (partition, offset): same offset means a redelivery, a new offset a re-send.

Real faults, and checking they landed

SIGKILL ends a process with no cleanup. docker pause freezes it; it wakes unaware time passed. A Toxiproxy blackhole keeps a connection open and drops the bytes; disabling a proxy instead resets connections, which fail fast: a different fault. The seed fixes the schedule, not the outcome.
Matters
A manipulation check, reading only orchestrator, broker and process state, confirms each A6 trial's fault took effect. A trial is void if not, and never because of what the checker found.

Positive and negative controls

A zero means something only if the harness can see a nonzero. A positive control runs a case that must produce the anomaly; a negative control, one that must produce none.
Like
Testing a smoke alarm with test smoke before trusting its silence.
Matters
A6's detection gate, run before the campaign, planted a duplicate and a loss on N only, ran no-fault trials on every stack and tested the checker on synthetic histories. It passed.

System views

Where a duplicate can enter

Two routes repeat a payment: the outbox relay re-reading after a crash, at a new offset, and redelivery to a restarted consumer, at the same offset. The producer's own retries are dropped by the broker. The key is checked last.

  • Client — assigns payment_id at the origin
  • payment-service — commits the source; sends in N, K, I
  • Kafka — payments topic
  • Ledger consumer key step — applies, commits Postgres, then its offset
  • source-db — the payment's record: the authority
  • Outbox relay — O+I only: Debezium
  • ledger-db — balances, apply log; key in I, O+I
  1. Client → payment-service: HTTP
  2. payment-service → source-db: commit
  3. payment-service → Kafka: send: retries deduped
  4. source-db → Outbox relay: outbox rows
  5. Outbox relay → Kafka: re-send: new offset
  6. Kafka → Ledger consumer: redeliver: same offset
  7. Ledger consumer → ledger-db: apply, COMMIT

One batch, two commits

Every A6 consumer commits Postgres, then its Kafka progress. A crash in between is the commit window: the ledger has the batch, the offset does not.

  • Poll a batch — records with (partition, offset)
  • Apply in Postgres — balances, apply log; key check in I, O+I
  • Postgres COMMIT — the ledger now has the batch
  • Commit offset — N, I, O+I: commitSync; K: commitTransaction
  • Crash in between key step — ledger committed, offset not
  • Restart — resumes at the last committed offset
  • Same batch again key step — applied twice unless the key skips it
  1. Poll a batch → Apply in Postgres
  2. Apply in Postgres → Postgres COMMIT
  3. Postgres COMMIT → Commit offset: then
  4. Postgres COMMIT → Crash in between: SIGKILL
  5. Crash in between → Restart
  6. Restart → Same batch again: redelivered

Recall check

Answer out loud first, then open.

A consumer is killed after its Postgres commit but before its offset commit. What happens on restart?

It resumes from the last committed offset, so the batch is redelivered and applied again: an extra apply at the same (partition, offset), unless the sink checks a key.

Why is a Kafka offset a bad idempotency key under an outbox relay?

After a crash the relay re-reads and re-sends, and the copy lands at a new offset: to an offset key it is a new message. A key assigned at the origin is the same on every copy.

K commits its offsets inside a Kafka transaction. How can it still apply a payment twice?

Its Postgres commit is outside the transaction. Die after it and before commitTransaction: the transaction aborts on restart, the batch is redelivered, the Postgres commit stands.

A zombie consumer wakes with a batch in hand. Which fencing stops it, and does that stop the duplicate?

Generation fencing, at its offset commit. Its Postgres write is another system's commit and has already happened, so fencing stops the Kafka commit, not the ledger write.

Why is a timed-out request recorded as info, not fail?

It may have reached the service and been recorded. Calling it fail would turn real deliveries into false anomalies; in A6 the source database resolves it, which a real client cannot do.

A cell shows zero duplicates. What must hold before that zero means anything?

The checker must be able to see a duplicate (a positive control that produced one), and the fault must have taken effect in each trial (its manipulation check).
